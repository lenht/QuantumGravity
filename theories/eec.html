Elementary Spaces/Theories/Entropic Expansion Cosmology

Theory 4 of 6 · first proposed 19 May 2013

The universe expands to keep entropy constant

Space Particle Dualism and the Similar-Worlds Interpretation supply the machinery; this chapter puts it to work on cosmology. Black hole entropy grows with the square of mass, so if the universe's entropy density is to stay fixed forever, space itself has to expand — faster and faster, to keep pace. It's a single, compact idea that reaches unusually far: run it all the way through and it removes the need for dark energy and inflation, and yields Darmos's most consequential number, a universe not 13.8 billion years old but roughly 42 trillion. This page walks through the mechanism, the derivation of that figure, and five independent lines of evidence Darmos offers to support it.

Where the theory starts

A constant, not a beginning

Standard cosmology treats the early universe's low entropy as a brute fact that has to be assumed rather than explained: the universe simply started in an exceptionally special, low-entropy state, and everything since has been entropy climbing back toward a more ordinary value. Entropic Expansion Cosmology tries to make that special starting condition disappear altogether, by proposing that it's entropy density — entropy per unit of volume — that's fixed, not total entropy, and never was anything else.

Almost all of the universe's entropy lives in black holes, and a black hole's entropy grows with the square of its mass. As black holes across the universe slowly grow — by accretion, by merging with one another — total cosmic entropy climbs far faster than a fixed volume could absorb while keeping its density constant. Darmos's proposal is that space itself expands to compensate, and because entropy is growing quadratically, the expansion needed to keep pace has to accelerate too — matching the observed acceleration of cosmic expansion without needing a separate dark-energy field to drive it.

What "entropy" means here

The chapter borrows a definition Darmos introduced earlier: the Similar-Worlds Interpretation already treats a superposition as a small cloud of worlds too similar to be told apart. Entropy, on this same picture, is recast as a literal count of how many such indistinguishable worlds a given macroscopic state corresponds to — not a separate statistical bookkeeping device bolted on afterward, but the same cloud, measured. A high-entropy state is one consistent with an enormous number of indistinguishable micro-arrangements; a low-entropy one, very few. That redefinition is what lets the chapter treat "entropy density" as a physically real, world-counting quantity rather than a purely thermodynamic abstraction.

A problem that stops needing solving

One immediate payoff: the horizon problem. Standard cosmology has to explain why distant regions of the cosmic microwave background, which — under ordinary expansion history — never had time to exchange light and settle into thermal agreement, nonetheless show nearly identical temperatures; the usual fix is a brief period of faster-than-light inflation early on, smoothing everything out before normal expansion resumed. Under entropic expansion there's nothing to smooth out in the first place: entropy density has always held its constant value, so the universe was never in the special, far-from-equilibrium state inflation is invoked to erase. The problem doesn't get solved so much as it never arises.

Checking the mechanism nearby

Local expansion, and why it should be nearly invisible

If expansion is entropy density catching up with black-hole growth, it should be strongest where entropy is growing fastest and weakest where it isn't — and a dense, gravitationally bound region like our own galaxy cluster is exactly where growth, and so expansion, should be smallest. That's a falsifiable difference from standard cosmology, which treats the expansion rate as the same everywhere, including inside gravitationally bound systems, just usually too small there to notice.

Darmos runs that difference against data in a few ways. A 225-galaxy redshift survey of the local group checks for "outlaw" galaxies — ones whose measured redshift is anomalously low, as would happen if a small, uniform local expansion were being partly cancelled out by the galaxy's own peculiar motion toward us. Standard, homogeneous expansion predicts some outlaws should turn up; entropic expansion, with local expansion suppressed by the region's low entropy growth, predicts essentially none — and the survey finds none. Decades of tracked trajectory data from the Pioneer 10/11 probes and Voyager 1, along with long-baseline observations of comets and close binary star systems, are checked the same way, for any secular drift consistent with even a very small uniform expansion acting inside the solar system or a bound stellar pair; none is found, which Darmos treats as consistent with — not proof of — an expansion rate that's suppressed almost to nothing in dense, low-entropy surroundings.

Is "expansion" even a separate thing from recession velocity?

Standard cosmology usually distinguishes cosmological redshift — space itself stretching, described as "metric expansion" — from an ordinary Doppler shift caused by objects actually moving apart. Darmos pushes back on treating that as a deep physical distinction rather than a choice of coordinates: the two descriptions are mathematically interchangeable, so insisting that only the metric-expansion picture applies, and only above some large cosmological scale, is itself an assumption rather than a settled result. That's the chapter's justification for treating a small amount of genuine local expansion as a live possibility worth testing at all, rather than ruling it out by definition the way a strict metric-expansion-only reading would.

Reinterpreting the missing mass

Dark matter, recast as burned-out stars

Galaxy rotation curves stay flat well past the point where visible matter alone should let them fall off, which is standard cosmology's central piece of evidence for an unseen particle species. In a universe tens of trillions of years old, Darmos argues, there's a far more mundane explanation available: stellar populations have had time to run through hundreds of generations, and the great majority of stars that have ever formed have simply burned out — white dwarfs, neutron stars, and stellar-mass black holes, contributing plenty of gravitating mass and no light at all.

The same reframing extends to two other puzzles. So-called "dark matter galaxies" — objects like VIRGOHI21, detected only through their gravitational and radio-emission signature, with no visible starlight — become ordinary galaxies whose entire stellar population has already burned out, not a new class of object built from exotic matter. And cosmic voids, the vast, apparently empty regions between galaxy clusters, become galaxy clusters that have simply exhausted their gas supply and gone fully dark, rather than genuine gaps in the universe's structure.

Matching the number against the Milky Way

Worked through for our own galaxy, Darmos puts 89.75–94.26% of the Milky Way's mass in non-luminous form with a 15% gas fraction, and 90.15–94.49% with 10% gas — roughly the commonly cited ~90%. The range spans mainstream total-mass estimates of 0.8–1.5×1012 solar masses. It implies a star-birth rate of only about 0.05–0.10 stars per year (roughly 0.02–0.04 solar masses per year), about a hundred times below the 1–3 solar masses per year usually quoted from direct measurements. Darmos quotes about 3 per year for the standard figure and, in his own words, treats such a rate as something assumed from the age of the universe rather than measured directly; a much lower rate is what you'd expect if star formation has been running continuously, but slowly, across hundreds of generations spread over tens of trillions of years rather than compressed into 13.8 billion.

The ~90% is not independent of the total mass assumed. A recomputation for this guide reproduces Darmos's numbers (2.949×1011 stellar black holes; 85.47% and 99.4% dark with only black holes and with dwarfs added), but the birth rate is itself set as the total mass divided by 0.36 solar masses times 4.213×1013 years, so the dark fraction follows from the mainstream total mass and the remnant fraction. It shows that the old-universe model can accommodate about 90% non-luminous mass, not that it predicts it.

Where galaxies' seeds come from

Primordial black holes, from a crystal of exotic matter

Space Particle Dualism's own account of exotic matter — particles with both parts of their complex mass-energy negative — gives it negative inertial mass, which means it repels its own kind rather than attracting it. That repulsion, on Darmos's account, locks exotic matter into a single rigid, universe-spanning lattice: a Wigner crystal, and the very first structure to exist. Ordinary matter is then drawn toward the lattice points, flipping the local net mass positive and triggering the crystal's collapse into what becomes a sea of primordial black holes — the seeds every later galaxy grew around.

Darmos calculates a minimum mass for these primordial black holes directly: setting a black hole's own volume equal to the volume that many neutrons would occupy at ordinary nuclear density gives a threshold of about 9.80×1030 kg, or roughly 4.9 solar masses — below that, there simply isn't enough matter, packed at realistic density, to close a horizon around it in the first place.

Why protons outnumber neutrons nine to one

The present-day universe's matter is overwhelmingly protons rather than neutrons, by roughly nine to one. Darmos's proposed explanation ties back to that same collapse: neutrons, needed in disproportionate numbers to encapsulate and neutralize exotic-matter lattice points as the crystal broke apart, were selectively consumed in the process, leaving free protons in the surplus that defines today's ratio — a byproduct of how the crystal came apart, rather than a separately-tuned initial condition.

Revisiting two of general relativity's strangest predictions

Black holes without Hawking radiation, and no true singularity

General relativity's black holes come with two famously strange features: a genuine event horizon, a one-way surface nothing can cross back out of, and (once quantum effects are added) a slow leak of Hawking radiation that should eventually evaporate them entirely. Darmos rejects both, for related reasons rooted in the rest of this framework.

On Chromogravity's account, gravity is sourced by quark count rather than by mass or energy as such — and photons, carrying no quarks, exert no gravity and can carry none away either. Hawking radiation is overwhelmingly photons, so if gravity is genuinely quark-sourced, there's no mechanism left for that radiation to actually shrink a black hole's gravitating mass, whatever else it might carry off. What forms instead of a true event horizon is only an apparent, or coherence, horizon — a boundary in how information can be exchanged, not a literal one-way membrane in space itself. Darmos points to horizonless alternatives — Mitra's Eternally Collapsing Objects (1998), Houghton's separate argument that Hawking radiation halts the collapse, and Leiter and Robertson's 2003 extension of Mitra's model to Magnetospheric Eternally Collapsing Objects (Darmos is himself inconsistent on who coined the “magnetic” version, crediting Mitra in one section and Leiter and Robertson in another) — and to observed magnetic fields around compact objects like the quasar Q0957+561 as the kind of evidence a true, surface-less event horizon shouldn't easily allow for, since a horizon has nothing for a magnetic field to actually anchor to. These are real proposals in the literature, which gives the idea some company.

The same "only differences matter" principle used on the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page to explain why a perfectly uniform vacuum energy exerts no pull rules out a true Big Bang singularity the same way: if it's density differences, not density itself, that cause time dilation and curvature, then an early universe of enormous but perfectly uniform density would curve nothing at all, however extreme its raw density might have been — removing the reason to expect an infinitely curved starting point in the first place.

Darmos's own summary answer to the singularity problem is a different one from that Big Bang half, and it concerns black holes. A collapsing star's centre has the highest escape velocity, so it reaches the light barrier first and freezes in time, while the outer layers keep collapsing; that creates density differences which make escape velocities equal throughout and stops a singularity forming, and infinite time dilation means no true event horizon forms either. Darmos gives the reason as the existence of a preferred frame — the absolute frame he says quantum mechanics needs, for which he floats Earth itself as a candidate, citing the microwave background's north–south asymmetry as not a Doppler effect. The Chromogravity page covers how that sits with the past-light-cone picture on the Similar-Worlds page.

Where the evidence gets most extreme

Entanglement, the Fermi paradox, and cosmic-scale observation

This section goes further than anything else on this site. The Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page already noted that claims of a mind nudging a single photon in a lab rest on contested parapsychology evidence, chiefly Dean Radin's. Here Darmos extends the same kind of claim from a lab bench up to the large-scale structure of the observable universe itself — galaxy-distribution patterns, the cosmic microwave background's own anomalies. That's a difference of scale, not just of degree, and it deserves to be named plainly rather than treated as a routine continuation of the earlier chapter's claims.

The chapter's least extreme piece is a fairly ordinary entanglement argument, not a parapsychology claim, and it's Darmos's proposed answer to the Fermi paradox: why, in a universe this large and this old, we haven't detected any trace of another civilization. On this framework, consciousness only entangles with, and only ever collapses the wavefunction for, the parts of the universe already causally and quantum-mechanically linked to it. An independent alien civilization, if one exists, would sit inside its own separate "island of entanglement" — permanently outside ours, and so permanently undetectable to us, not because it's far away but because there's no shared collapse event connecting the two islands at all. That is the mechanism behind Darmos's claim that we're alone: consciousness only exists where it's entangled with ours.

Everything past this point leans on the same contested parapsychology evidence already noted on the SWI page, now applied at cosmological scale. Treat what follows as Darmos's own extreme edge, not as independent confirmation of anything.

Three further, considerably more extreme claims build on that entanglement idea:

  • CMB and large-scale-structure anomalies. The microwave background's "axis of evil" alignment and cold-spot positions, which Darmos ties to Earth's equinox and ecliptic planes, are reframed as consequences of observation centered on Earth, within this framework — rather than as unexplained statistical curiosities the standard model has to shrug off. The concentric shells that Hartnett reports in galaxy distributions (roughly 250 million light-years apart in standard distances) were first read the same way and linked to the Sun's roughly 240-million-year galactic orbit; Darmos then raised his own objections to that reading, and his current summary attributes the shells to baryon acoustic oscillations in an emergent universe instead, keeping observation-driven retrocausality for the north–south asymmetry seen in both galaxies and the microwave background. That BAO fit uses an initial temperature chosen to match the observed scale (see the full-age section).
  • "Abundance of future observation." Darmos's own name for a proposed retrocausal effect: regions of the universe that will be observed more, at some point in the future, are predicted to show higher structure and density now, with the causal arrow running backward from that future observation to the present structure. Darmos says this explains the cold spots and the north–south asymmetry but not the small density fluctuations.
  • Cosmokinesis. An extension of the same Radin-style psychokinesis claims discussed on the SWI page — the roughly 51%-versus-chance random-number-generator studies — up to cosmological scale, with intention or observation proposed to nudge photon density within the cosmic microwave background itself. Darmos calculates that about 38 minutes of focused intention per night would produce the observed hot- and cold-spot effect size.

Darmos himself frames three of the cosmic microwave background's observed acoustic-oscillation periodicities as unexplained by ordinary sound waves in the early plasma, leaving room for speculative mechanisms like these — but "unexplained by one mechanism" is a much weaker claim than "explained by this one," and none of the three claims above have any standing outside Darmos's own argument.

From mechanism to a headline number

The age of the CMB: deriving 42 trillion years

This is the chapter's centerpiece, and the number the rest of the site already leans on: the homepage's own timeline and formula-card entries, and Charge Mass Dualism's aside about a "16-trillion-year dark epoch," both assume the figure this section derives.

The starting ingredients are conventional: the 2019 Riess et al. measurement of the Hubble constant, , and a decoupling temperature of 2,900 K, corresponding to a redshift of . What's unconventional is the expansion history connecting those numbers to an age: because entropic expansion's rate depends on entropy density rather than following the standard model's own cooling curve, converting a redshift into an actual light-travel time requires a completely different distance-redshift relationship, worked out in this chapter as a new constant: . That constant is exactly what the homepage's own "Time–redshift equation" formula card already shows as — this section is where that card's derivation actually happens. Running the decoupling redshift through it, with a small correction for the initial expansion rate , gives a post-decoupling age of approximately 4.2130×1013 years. Darmos's two careful versions of that calculation (4.21301047 and 4.21301095×1013 years) agree to seven digits, which is why the site elsewhere just calls this ≈42.13 trillion years. The simpler approximation Darmos prints, (4.21456 ± 0.0808)×1013 years, differs from them in the fourth digit and carries an uncertainty of about ±0.8 trillion years from the Hubble constant.

A critique of the standard model's own first second

The chapter closes this section with a pointed methodological observation rather than a new number: most of what's popularly presented as "the early universe" — cosmic inflation, baryogenesis, the quark-gluon epoch — is really a set of claims about extreme, untested high-energy particle physics, compressed into a fraction of a second, and then presented to the public as established cosmological history rather than as speculative particle theory. Whatever one makes of the 42-trillion-year figure itself, Darmos argues that distinction — particle physics dressed up as settled cosmology — is worth keeping in mind when reading almost any popular account of the universe's first moments, on either side of this disagreement.

Past decoupling, to the very beginning

The full age of the universe: 58 trillion years

The post-decoupling figure above only covers time since the cosmic microwave background formed. To reach a full age, Darmos needs an independent way to measure entropy density directly, rather than working forward from a known temperature — and turns to a chart of about 70 black holes with measured masses (64 of them at z < 0.6), charting each one's mass, redshift, and implied entropy density.

That survey alone gives a wide range: depending on how a gap in the data — a "redshift desert" with essentially no charted supermassive black holes between z = 0.5 and z = 2 — is handled, the implied initial temperature ranges from 3,418 to 11,712 K, and the full age of the universe correspondingly from about 49.66 to 170.3 trillion years. That's the method's honest uncertainty band on its own, before anything else narrows it down. (An even earlier, now-superseded estimate, from before this survey existed, put the figure at roughly 1.08×1017 years — Darmos himself notes that number as wrong once the supermassive-black-hole survey refined the method.)

A check of the arithmetic, done for this guide, finds it sound: the chart's entropy column equals 0.707 times the Newtonian Bekenstein–Hawking value for every row (the mass rescaling of 0.841 that the chromogravity chapter predicts), the z < 1 entropies sum to 2.11×1076 J/K as Darmos has it, and Darmos's temperature relation reproduces 11,712 K from the chart's entropy density. The weak point is what goes in: the chart is a catalogue of black holes with measured masses, treated as the whole population. A rough outside comparison, using a standard local black-hole mass density of about 4×105 solar masses per cubic megaparsec, suggests that even 10-5–10-4 billion-solar-mass holes per cubic megaparsec would give an entropy density 103–104 times Darmos's and raise Ti by a factor of 10–40. On that reading, the 3,418–11,712 K band reflects how complete the catalogue is more than the physics.

One tentative pattern inside that same survey: average redshift rises with black-hole mass class — for black holes around 1010 solar masses, versus for black holes around 106 solar masses. Darmos offers this as tentative evidence for anisotropic, entropy-driven expansion tracking mass directly, while being explicit that an ordinary observational selection effect — heavier black holes being easier to detect at greater distances in the first place — is an equally live alternative explanation he hasn't ruled out. The class averages Darmos lists (0.0076, 0.0176, 1.006, 2.074 and 2.132) also rise in a step between 107 and 108 solar masses, a factor of 57, rather than as a steady proportionality.

Narrowing the range with baryonic acoustic oscillations

To pick one value out of that wide range, the chapter turns to baryonic acoustic oscillations — the faint periodic imprint of early sound waves, still visible in how galaxies are distributed today. Setting the initial temperature to predicts a BAO periodicity of 404,410,004 light-years, extremely close to the smallest observed BAO periodicity reported by Hartnett & Hirano (2008) of 404,175,047 light-years. That match is what Darmos settles on as his final figure: a full age of approximately 5.843×1013 years, or ≈58 trillion years — the same number behind Charge Mass Dualism's aside about a 16-trillion-year gap between the CMB's formation and today (58 minus 42).

Worth naming plainly, since it's a different kind of weakness than the "successive-correction-chain" caveat used elsewhere on this site (see Charge Mass Dualism's muon and tauon mass predictions): this is a tuned match, not an independent prediction. Ti = 4,021 K wasn't derived from anything else — it was chosen specifically because it reproduces one observed number almost exactly. A single parameter fit to a single data point is a different, and in some ways simpler, weakness than several parameters fit in sequence, but it's still weaker evidence than a genuine blind prediction at the same precision. The honest starting point is really the wide, independently-derived 49.7–170 trillion-year range from the supermassive-black-hole survey alone; BAO is being used here to pick one value out of that range after the fact, not to test the method from outside it. A recomputation of Darmos's formula, done for this guide, adds two points. First, the fit is steep: the predicted BAO scale runs from 43% of Hartnett's 404 million light-years at Ti = 3,418 K (age 49.66 trillion years) to about 5.43 billion light-years at 11,712 K (170.3 trillion), a factor of roughly 30 across the method's own band, so matching to 0.2% needs Ti within about 0.2% (an exact match comes at 4,012.6 K against Darmos's 4,021 K). That means the age is pinned tightly if the mechanism is right. Second, the mechanism is the sound speed of a non-relativistic gas (√(γkBT/mp), with γ = 5/3), whereas the standard BAO calculation uses the much faster photon–baryon plasma.

What else has to be true, if the age is real

Living in an old universe: consistency checks

A 42- or 58-trillion-year age isn't free-standing — it has to fit alongside other things astronomers already measure. Darmos runs several such checks, and it's a fair sign of a serious theory that he goes looking for them.

Where does a redshifted photon's lost energy go?

Cosmological redshift means a photon loses energy as it travels across an expanding universe — a fact standard cosmology already accepts, and doesn't always agree on how to interpret, since energy conservation is normally defined relative to a fixed frame that an expanding universe doesn't straightforwardly provide. Darmos argues from an absolute-frame perspective that this loss is real and irreversible — energy actually leaves the observable system, rather than being conserved in some subtler bookkeeping sense — and treats that one-way loss as consistent with, and evidence for, a genuinely one-directional flow of cosmic time, rather than as a bookkeeping puzzle to be explained away.

Do the heavy elements have enough time to form?

Iron and elements heavier than it are made almost exclusively in supernovae and other violent stellar deaths, so their present-day abundance is a clock of sorts: enough stellar generations have to have lived and died to produce what we observe. In only 13.8 billion years, fitting the observed abundance requires a fairly brisk, tightly scheduled sequence of stellar generations. A universe tens of trillions of years old removes that time pressure entirely — there's ample time for the observed abundance to accumulate at a far more leisurely pace, which Darmos treats as a point in the old-age model's favor rather than a coincidence needing its own separate explanation.

Where are the first-generation stars?

Standard cosmology predicts that the universe's very first stars — metal-free, built purely from primordial hydrogen and helium — should still be detectable somewhere, and none has ever been conclusively found. On the old-universe reading, that's not a problem with early star-formation theory at all: with tens of trillions of years having passed, every star from that original, metal-free population has long since burned out or been chemically enriched by earlier generations of supernovae, leaving nothing pristine left to find.

From formula to a direct data comparison

Testing it: Type Ia supernova luminosities

Type Ia supernovae are one of cosmology's standard candles: they're thought to detonate at a consistent mass — the Chandrasekhar limit — and so should reach a fairly consistent peak brightness, which lets astronomers infer distance from how bright one looks. Darmos's test is subtler than a fixed candle: recompute each supernova's distance under the two distance-redshift relationships, rescale the catalogue's own apparent magnitudes to match, and see whether the inverse-square law holds across the sample.

Filtering a sample of 8,830 Type Ia supernovae down to a usable set, Darmos finds their average peak magnitude comes out to −18.851 under standard GR distances and −21.2627 under this chapter's entropic distances. He reads the first as too dim: he takes −19.3 as the floor for a single white dwarf detonation, and 46.42% of the sample falls below that under GR distances, against only about 2% under entropic distances. He reads the larger distances as meaning the supernovae must also be more powerful, so the farthest ones should show record luminosities, and super-Chandrasekhar mergers can exceed the standard value. An average of −21.26 is about two magnitudes brighter than a standard Type Ia, which makes this a test of inverse-square consistency, not of a fixed standard candle.

Plotted directly, distance against apparent magnitude, the entropic-distance data sits tightly along the inverse-square law's expected line across the whole sample. The GR-distance data tracks it well at low redshift but drifts increasingly off it beyond about z ≈ 0.2, opening a gap of roughly 7 billion light-years between where the two trendlines predict a magnitude-25 object should sit. Darmos also notes that his own data may be slightly off because the catalogue's Hubble values vary.

This test rests on the distance baseline, and a check made for this guide finds that baseline doing most of the work. In Darmos's account, the “GR distance” is a light-travel (look-back) distance, while the catalogue magnitudes it is compared with are computed from the luminosity distance. Against the GR luminosity distance instead (flat, H0 = 70.4, Ωm = 0.3), the brightness factors Darmos lists for the five most distant supernovae (175.6, 58.5, 50.4, 52.0 and 44.2, each the squared ratio of entropic to light-travel distance) become 1.9, 2.5, 2.7, 2.6 and 2.8: a shift of 0.7–1.1 magnitudes, not 4.1–5.6. Three consequences follow. The drift away from the inverse-square line beyond z ≈ 0.2 coincides with where light-travel and luminosity distance separate, so it is expected from the choice of distance rather than from the data. At low redshift the shift is fixed by construction (the entropic distance is 3c/H0 at 74.03 against c/H0 at 70.4, or 5 log10 2.85 ≈ 2.3 magnitudes), so moving the whole sample about 2.4 magnitudes brighter says little on its own. And with the proper baseline the shift shrinks from about 2.2 magnitudes at z = 0.1 to about 0.7 at z = 3.9, so distant supernovae would come out fainter than nearby ones under Darmos's linear distance law — the opposite of the record luminosities Darmos expects.

Two smaller points. Darmos writes that a factor of 3 in distance is “at least” a factor of 3 in luminosity, but the inverse-square law makes it about 8–9. And −19.3 is the usual average peak for a Type Ia, not a floor, so about half of a normal sample sits dimmer than it (catalogues also mix in known subluminous types); the “46% below the minimum” figure shouldn't be read as a physical impossibility.

Four checks against the oldest things we can actually measure

Testing it: white dwarfs, black dwarfs, and two lone objects

White dwarfs: an age nothing exceeds

White dwarfs cool at a well-understood, predictable rate, which makes their measured temperature a direct age clock. Filtering the Montreal White Dwarf Database down to 33,851 entries above the hydrogen-fusion mass floor of 0.075 solar masses, Darmos finds that 33,808 of them — 99.87% — are older than the standard model's 13.8-billion-year age for the universe, which is already an uncomfortable number for the standard picture. Against the 42-trillion-year figure derived above, by contrast, zero exceed it.

Two objects sit well apart from the rest of the sample: SDSS J142402.68+061206.5, at 35.4 trillion years, and SDSS J161213.77+163906.3, at 35.7 trillion years — 85% of the full post-decoupling age. Darmos calls them pretty much outliers: a 13-trillion-year gap separates them from the main body of white dwarfs, at about 23–25 trillion years, which he thinks are mostly invisible black dwarfs. The separate six-trillion-year gap between the two and the 42-trillion-year age he puts down to stars not forming immediately after decoupling (a November 2025 revision that reversed an earlier black-dwarf explanation).

A separate minimum-mass check cuts the other way against the standard model: under a 13.8-billion-year age, only 36% of the white dwarfs in the database are even massive enough to have had time to form at all — implying the other 64% "shouldn't exist" on that timeline. Under the 42-trillion-year age, the minimum mass needed drops to just 0.062 solar masses, and essentially every white dwarf on record is consistent with having had time to form.

Black dwarfs: a near-term falsifiable prediction

A black dwarf is what a white dwarf becomes once it finally finishes cooling to the temperature of its surroundings — invisible in ordinary light, but only if the universe is old enough for any to have formed at all; under the standard 13.8-billion-year age, none should exist anywhere yet. Darmos catalogues 16 documented candidates: invisible companions inferred from their gravitational effect on a visible partner star in double-star systems, each too massive to be a planet and invisible in ordinary light. Darmos is more hedged than that framing suggests: four of the invisible partners get no likely temperature (three are slightly lighter than their visible partners, and the fourth is a pulsar companion whose age can't be estimated), he says his candidates are not necessarily colder than visible white dwarfs, and he says black dwarfs cannot be used to date the full age of the universe. HD 188112–b and PTF1 J0823+0819–b are his two near-term observable cases.

HD 188112–b is the headline candidate, with PTF1 J0823+0819–b the other near-term case: Darmos frames a direct detection as an immediate, near-term test that current instruments could plausibly settle.

Darmos is direct about what a confirmed detection would mean: it would become the first directly observed black dwarf, and — in his own words — hand "the standard model of cosmology yet another death blow." A separate consistency check inside the same database finds that of 27 white dwarfs carrying a binary ("a/b") designation, 24 (89%) have an invisible companion — close to the roughly 90% non-luminous-matter figure calculated earlier in this page for the Milky Way as a whole.

The coldest known neutron star

PSR J2144−3933, at 1.97 ± 0.04 solar masses, has never been detected optically at all — only an upper limit on its temperature, 42,000 K, is known. That limit alone implies a minimum age of about 2.945×1012 years, on the order of 3 trillion, which Darmos sets against roughly 10 million years, his figure for an average pulsar's radio lifetime (not a cooling-theory prediction). He also quotes the discovery team's own puzzlement (M. D. Young, 1999) about the same pulsar, whose 8.5-second rotation period gave the discovery paper its title: the remark concerns its radio emission — a pulsar spinning this slowly should not be emitting a radio beam — not its cooling.

The cosmic neutrino background's temperature

Darmos predicts a cosmic neutrino background temperature of 1.96385 ± 0.00057 K, against standard Big Bang cosmology's own, independently derived prediction of 1.95 K. That precise figure comes from comparing photon redshifts at the border of the past (T = 4,021 K, the same fitted temperature used for the BAO match above) and at decoupling (z = 1,063); a rougher version, from the ratio of the 42- and 58-trillion-year ages, gives only about 1.97 K. Darmos is candid that the near-agreement with 1.95 K is "purely coincidental" — the standard figure comes from electron-positron annihilation heating, a different mechanism entirely — and he says so plainly rather than presenting the match as confirmation of anything. It's a small, honest moment worth noting on its own terms.

Closing speculation, at the edges of an eon

The end and the beginning

This is the second-highest caveat bar on this page, after the entanglement section above. Both pieces below reach outside mainstream science, and the first leans on ideas — morphic resonance and a reincarnation-like continuity between cosmic eons — that this site's Conscious Set Theory page (Chapter 5) is where Darmos actually tries to unpack the mechanism, rather than here.

The last observer, and the next eon's first

If a conscious observer's own entanglement is what keeps a region of the universe determinate, what happens once the very last conscious observer in a given cosmic eon dies? Darmos's answer reaches forward into material this page won't try to fully unpack: he proposes that the last observer of one eon and the first observer of the next are linked by morphic resonance — biologist Rupert Sheldrake's proposed nonlocal tendency for patterns to recur — so that the next eon's starting quantum state isn't arbitrary, but inherited from the one that just ended. Darmos repeatedly points ahead to where this gets developed properly ("see chapter 5.1," "reincarnation will be discussed in chapter 5.4"), and this page follows that same lead: the puzzle and the proposed link are stated here because they're this chapter's own material, but the mechanism itself is left for the Conscious Set Theory page to cover, the same way Charge Mass Dualism's page leans on Space Particle Dualism's critical photon energy for its W/Z-boson mass estimate rather than re-deriving that energy itself.

A more concrete companion: why the Moon looks the way it does

A second, less extreme piece of speculation closes the chapter with something closer to a testable number. Darmos proposes that life appears as early as it's geologically possible for it to appear, reasoning that the pre-conscious past of a planet only becomes "real," on this framework's own terms, retroactively — once some later consciousness actually emerges to entangle with it. That reframes the usual "rare Earth" observations — the Moon's apparent size matching the Sun's to about 97.25% precision, Earth's unusually low orbital eccentricity, its magnetic field, a Jupiter-sized debris-sweeper further out in the solar system — not as anthropic selection (we notice these coincidences only because we're here to notice them) but as evidence of the same morphic resonance between eons proposed above: conditions tuned, across cycles, to let consciousness arise as promptly as physically possible each time. It's a more concrete, number-anchored claim than the "last observer" puzzle above, but it rests on exactly the same speculative mechanism.
