Theory 4 of 6 · first proposed 19 May 2013

The universe expands to keep entropy constant

Space Particle Dualism and the Similar-Worlds Interpretation supply the machinery; this chapter puts it to work on cosmology. Black hole entropy grows with the square of mass, so if the universe's entropy density is to stay fixed forever, space itself has to expand — faster and faster, to keep pace. Run that single idea all the way through, and it produces the book's most consequential number: a universe not 13.8 billion years old, but roughly 42 trillion. This page covers the mechanism, the derivation of that figure, and five independent lines of evidence the book offers in its support.

Where the theory starts

A constant, not a beginning

Standard cosmology treats the early universe's low entropy as a brute fact that has to be assumed rather than explained: the universe simply started in an exceptionally special, low-entropy state, and everything since has been entropy climbing back toward a more ordinary value. Entropic Expansion Cosmology tries to make that special starting condition disappear altogether, by proposing that it's entropy density — entropy per unit of volume — that's fixed, not total entropy, and never was anything else.

Almost all of the universe's entropy lives in black holes, and a black hole's entropy grows with the square of its mass. As black holes across the universe slowly grow — by accretion, by merging with one another — total cosmic entropy climbs far faster than a fixed volume could absorb while keeping its density constant. The book's proposal is that space itself expands to compensate, and because entropy is growing quadratically, the expansion needed to keep pace has to accelerate too — matching the observed acceleration of cosmic expansion without needing a separate dark-energy field to drive it.

What "entropy" means here

The chapter borrows a definition from earlier in the book: the Similar-Worlds Interpretation already treats a superposition as a small cloud of worlds too similar to be told apart. Entropy, on this same picture, is recast as a literal count of how many such indistinguishable worlds a given macroscopic state corresponds to — not a separate statistical bookkeeping device bolted on afterward, but the same cloud, measured. A high-entropy state is one consistent with an enormous number of indistinguishable micro-arrangements; a low-entropy one, very few. That redefinition is what lets the chapter treat "entropy density" as a physically real, world-counting quantity rather than a purely thermodynamic abstraction.

A problem that stops needing solving

One immediate payoff: the horizon problem. Standard cosmology has to explain why distant regions of the cosmic microwave background, which — under ordinary expansion history — never had time to exchange light and settle into thermal agreement, nonetheless show nearly identical temperatures; the usual fix is a brief period of faster-than-light inflation early on, smoothing everything out before normal expansion resumed. Under entropic expansion there's nothing to smooth out in the first place: entropy density has always held its constant value, so the universe was never in the special, far-from-equilibrium state inflation is invoked to erase. The problem doesn't get solved so much as it never arises.

Checking the mechanism nearby

Local expansion, and why it should be nearly invisible

If expansion is entropy density catching up with black-hole growth, it should be strongest where entropy is growing fastest and weakest where it isn't — and a dense, gravitationally bound region like our own galaxy cluster is exactly where growth, and so expansion, should be smallest. That's a falsifiable difference from standard cosmology, which treats the expansion rate as the same everywhere, including inside gravitationally bound systems, just usually too small there to notice.

The book runs that difference against data in a few ways. A 225-galaxy redshift survey of the local group checks for "outlaw" galaxies — ones whose measured redshift is anomalously low, as would happen if a small, uniform local expansion were being partly cancelled out by the galaxy's own peculiar motion toward us. Standard, homogeneous expansion predicts some outlaws should turn up; entropic expansion, with local expansion suppressed by the region's low entropy growth, predicts essentially none — and the survey finds none. Decades of tracked trajectory data from the Pioneer 10/11 probes and Voyager 1, along with long-baseline observations of comets and close binary star systems, are checked the same way, for any secular drift consistent with even a very small uniform expansion acting inside the solar system or a bound stellar pair; none is found, which the book treats as consistent with — not proof of — an expansion rate that's suppressed almost to nothing in dense, low-entropy surroundings.

Is "expansion" even a separate thing from recession velocity?

Standard cosmology usually distinguishes cosmological redshift — space itself stretching, described as "metric expansion" — from an ordinary Doppler shift caused by objects actually moving apart. The book pushes back on treating that as a deep physical distinction rather than a choice of coordinates: the two descriptions are mathematically interchangeable, so insisting that only the metric-expansion picture applies, and only above some large cosmological scale, is itself an assumption rather than a settled result. That's the chapter's justification for treating a small amount of genuine local expansion as a live possibility worth testing at all, rather than ruling it out by definition the way a strict metric-expansion-only reading would.

Reinterpreting the missing mass

Dark matter, recast as burned-out stars

Galaxy rotation curves stay flat well past the point where visible matter alone should let them fall off, which is standard cosmology's central piece of evidence for an unseen particle species. In a universe tens of trillions of years old, the book argues, there's a far more mundane explanation available: stellar populations have had time to run through hundreds of generations, and the great majority of stars that have ever formed have simply burned out — white dwarfs, neutron stars, and stellar-mass black holes, contributing plenty of gravitating mass and no light at all.

The same reframing extends to two other puzzles. So-called "dark matter galaxies" — objects like VIRGOHI21, detected only through their gravitational and radio-emission signature, with no visible starlight — become ordinary galaxies whose entire stellar population has already burned out, not a new class of object built from exotic matter. And cosmic voids, the vast, apparently empty regions between galaxy clusters, become galaxy clusters that have simply exhausted their gas supply and gone fully dark, rather than genuine gaps in the universe's structure.

Matching the number against the Milky Way

Worked through for our own galaxy, the book's refined estimate puts 89.7–94.5% of the Milky Way's mass in non-luminous form, narrowing to almost exactly the commonly cited ~90% figure once the gas fraction used in the calculation is set to 10% rather than 15%. That pins the Milky Way's total mass at roughly 0.8×1012 solar masses and implies a star-formation rate of only about 0.05–0.09 new stars per year — far below the standard model's directly-fitted rate of roughly 7 per year — which is exactly what you'd expect if star formation has been running continuously, but slowly, across hundreds of generations spread over tens of trillions of years rather than compressed into 13.8 billion.

Where galaxies' seeds come from

Primordial black holes, from a crystal of exotic matter

Space Particle Dualism's own account of exotic matter — particles with both parts of their complex mass-energy negative — gives it negative inertial mass, which means it repels its own kind rather than attracting it. That repulsion, on the book's account, locks exotic matter into a single rigid, universe-spanning lattice: a Wigner crystal, and the very first structure to exist. Ordinary matter is then drawn toward the lattice points, flipping the local net mass positive and triggering the crystal's collapse into what becomes a sea of primordial black holes — the seeds every later galaxy grew around.

The book calculates a minimum mass for these primordial black holes directly: setting a black hole's own volume equal to the volume that many neutrons would occupy at ordinary nuclear density gives a threshold of about 9.80×1030 kg, or roughly 4.9 solar masses — below that, there simply isn't enough matter, packed at realistic density, to close a horizon around it in the first place.

Why protons outnumber neutrons nine to one

The present-day universe's matter is overwhelmingly protons rather than neutrons, by roughly nine to one. The book's proposed explanation ties back to that same collapse: neutrons, needed in disproportionate numbers to encapsulate and neutralize exotic-matter lattice points as the crystal broke apart, were selectively consumed in the process, leaving free protons in the surplus that defines today's ratio — a byproduct of how the crystal came apart, rather than a separately-tuned initial condition.

Revisiting two of general relativity's strangest predictions

Black holes without Hawking radiation, and no true singularity

General relativity's black holes come with two famously strange features: a genuine event horizon, a one-way surface nothing can cross back out of, and (once quantum effects are added) a slow leak of Hawking radiation that should eventually evaporate them entirely. The book rejects both, for related reasons rooted in the rest of this framework.

On Chromogravity's account, gravity is sourced by quark count rather than by mass or energy as such — and photons, carrying no quarks, exert no gravity and can carry none away either. Hawking radiation is overwhelmingly photons, so if gravity is genuinely quark-sourced, there's no mechanism left for that radiation to actually shrink a black hole's gravitating mass, whatever else it might carry off. What forms instead of a true event horizon is only an apparent, or coherence, horizon — a boundary in how information can be exchanged, not a literal one-way membrane in space itself. The book points to Eternally Collapsing Objects and Magnetospheric Eternally Collapsing Objects — a horizonless alternative to black holes proposed by Robertson and Leiter — and to observed magnetic fields around compact objects like the quasar Q0957+561 as the kind of evidence a true, surface-less event horizon shouldn't easily allow for, since a horizon has nothing for a magnetic field to actually anchor to.

The same "only differences matter" principle used on the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page to explain why a perfectly uniform vacuum energy exerts no pull rules out a true Big Bang singularity the same way: if it's density differences, not density itself, that cause time dilation and curvature, then an early universe of enormous but perfectly uniform density would curve nothing at all, however extreme its raw density might have been — removing the reason to expect an infinitely curved starting point in the first place.

Where the evidence gets most extreme

Entanglement, the Fermi paradox, and cosmic-scale observation

This section goes further than anything else on this site. The Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page already flagged claims of a mind nudging a single photon in a lab as resting on contested parapsychology evidence, chiefly Dean Radin's. Here the book extends the same kind of claim from a lab bench up to the large-scale structure of the observable universe itself — galaxy-distribution patterns, the cosmic microwave background's own anomalies. That's a difference of scale, not just of degree, and it deserves to be named plainly rather than treated as a routine continuation of the earlier chapter's claims.

The chapter's least extreme piece is a fairly ordinary entanglement argument, not a parapsychology claim, and it's the book's proposed answer to the Fermi paradox: why, in a universe this large and this old, we haven't detected any trace of another civilization. On this framework, consciousness only entangles with, and only ever collapses the wavefunction for, the parts of the universe already causally and quantum-mechanically linked to it. An independent alien civilization, if one exists, would sit inside its own separate "island of entanglement" — permanently outside ours, and so permanently undetectable to us, not because it's far away but because there's no shared collapse event connecting the two islands at all. It's this mechanism the homepage's own Fermi-paradox line ("We're alone: consciousness only exists where it's entangled with ours") is pointing at.

Everything past this point leans on the same contested parapsychology evidence already flagged on the SWI page, now applied at cosmological scale. Treat what follows as the book's own extreme edge, not as independent confirmation of anything.

Three further, considerably more extreme claims build on that entanglement idea:

  • CMB and large-scale-structure anomalies. Observed patterns like the microwave background's "axis of evil" alignment, and large-scale concentric-shell-like structure in galaxy distributions, are reframed as consequences of observation centered on Earth, within this framework — rather than as unexplained statistical curiosities the standard model has to shrug off.
  • "Abundance of future observation." The book's own name for a proposed retrocausal effect: regions of the universe that will be observed more, at some point in the future, are predicted to show higher structure and density now, with the causal arrow running backward from that future observation to the present structure.
  • Cosmokinesis. An extension of the same Radin-style psychokinesis claims discussed on the SWI page — the roughly 51%-versus-chance random-number-generator studies — up to cosmological scale, with intention or observation proposed to nudge photon density within the cosmic microwave background itself.

The book itself frames three of the cosmic microwave background's observed acoustic-oscillation periodicities as unexplained by ordinary sound waves in the early plasma, leaving room for speculative mechanisms like these — but "unexplained by one mechanism" is a much weaker claim than "explained by this one," and none of the three claims above have any standing outside the book's own argument.

From mechanism to a headline number

The age of the CMB: deriving 42 trillion years

This is the chapter's centerpiece, and the number the rest of the site already leans on: the homepage's own timeline and formula-card entries, and Charge Mass Dualism's aside about a "16-trillion-year dark epoch," both assume the figure this section derives.

The starting ingredients are conventional: the 2019 Riess et al. measurement of the Hubble constant, , and a decoupling temperature of 2,900 K, corresponding to a redshift of . What's unconventional is the expansion history connecting those numbers to an age: because entropic expansion's rate depends on entropy density rather than following the standard model's own cooling curve, converting a redshift into an actual light-travel time requires a completely different distance-redshift relationship, worked out in this chapter as a new constant: . That constant is exactly what the homepage's own "Time–redshift equation" formula card already shows as — this section is where that card's derivation actually happens. Running the decoupling redshift through it, with a small correction for the initial expansion rate , gives a post-decoupling age of approximately 4.2130×1013 years; a simpler approximation without that correction gives 4.21456×1013 years — the two agree to the first seven digits, which is why the site elsewhere just calls this ≈42.13 trillion years.

Set against a roughly 42-trillion-year CMB age, the book's own framing line is blunt: we've seen roughly 1% of the universe's history. Everything astronomy has ever directly observed, on this account, is a thin recent sliver.

A critique of the standard model's own first second

The chapter closes this section with a pointed methodological complaint rather than a new number: most of what's popularly presented as "the early universe" — cosmic inflation, baryogenesis, the quark-gluon epoch — is really a set of claims about extreme, untested high-energy particle physics, compressed into a fraction of a second, and then marketed to the public as established cosmological history rather than as speculative particle theory. Whatever one makes of the 42-trillion-year figure itself, the book argues that distinction — particle physics dressed up as settled cosmology — is worth keeping in mind when reading almost any popular account of the universe's first moments, on either side of this disagreement.

Past decoupling, to the very beginning

The full age of the universe: 58 trillion years

The post-decoupling figure above only covers time since the cosmic microwave background formed. To reach a full age, the book needs an independent way to measure entropy density directly, rather than working forward from a known temperature — and turns to a survey of roughly 60 supermassive black holes, charting each one's mass, redshift, and implied entropy density.

That survey alone gives a wide range: depending on how a gap in the data — a "redshift desert" with essentially no charted supermassive black holes between z = 0.5 and z = 2 — is handled, the implied initial temperature ranges from 3,418 to 11,712 K, and the full age of the universe correspondingly from about 49.66 to 170.3 trillion years. That's the method's honest uncertainty band on its own, before anything else narrows it down. (An even earlier, now-superseded estimate, from before this survey existed, put the figure at roughly 1.08×1017 years — the book itself flags that number as wrong once the supermassive-black-hole survey refined the method.)

One tentative pattern inside that same survey: average redshift scales with black-hole mass class — for black holes around 1010 solar masses, versus for black holes around 106 solar masses. The book offers this as tentative evidence for anisotropic, entropy-driven expansion tracking mass directly, while being explicit that an ordinary observational selection effect — heavier black holes being easier to detect at greater distances in the first place — is an equally live alternative explanation it hasn't ruled out.

Narrowing the range with baryonic acoustic oscillations

To pick one value out of that wide range, the chapter turns to baryonic acoustic oscillations — the faint periodic imprint of early sound waves, still visible in how galaxies are distributed today. Setting the initial temperature to predicts a BAO periodicity of 404,410,004 light-years, extremely close to the smallest observed BAO periodicity reported by Hartnett & Hirano (2008) of 404,175,047 light-years. That match is what the book settles on as its final figure: a full age of approximately 5.843×1013 years, or ≈58 trillion years — the same number behind Charge Mass Dualism's aside about a 16-trillion-year gap between the CMB's formation and today (58 minus 42).

Worth naming plainly, since it's a different kind of weakness than the "successive-correction-chain" caveat used elsewhere on this site (see Charge Mass Dualism's muon and tauon mass predictions): this is a tuned match, not an independent prediction. Ti = 4,021 K wasn't derived from anything else — it was chosen specifically because it reproduces one observed number almost exactly. A single parameter fit to a single data point is a different, and in some ways simpler, weakness than several parameters fit in sequence, but it's still weaker evidence than a genuine blind prediction at the same precision. The honest starting point is really the wide, independently-derived 49.7–170 trillion-year range from the supermassive-black-hole survey alone; BAO is being used here to pick one value out of that range after the fact, not to test the method from outside it.

What else has to be true, if the age is real

Living in an old universe: two consistency checks

A 42- or 58-trillion-year age isn't free-standing — it has to fit alongside other things astronomers already measure. The book runs two such checks.

Where does a redshifted photon's lost energy go?

Cosmological redshift means a photon loses energy as it travels across an expanding universe — a fact standard cosmology already accepts, and doesn't always agree on how to interpret, since energy conservation is normally defined relative to a fixed frame that an expanding universe doesn't straightforwardly provide. The book argues from an absolute-frame perspective that this loss is real and irreversible — energy actually leaves the observable system, rather than being conserved in some subtler bookkeeping sense — and treats that one-way loss as consistent with, and evidence for, a genuinely one-directional flow of cosmic time, rather than as a bookkeeping puzzle to be explained away.

Do the heavy elements have enough time to form?

Iron and elements heavier than it are made almost exclusively in supernovae and other violent stellar deaths, so their present-day abundance is a clock of sorts: enough stellar generations have to have lived and died to produce what we observe. In only 13.8 billion years, fitting the observed abundance requires a fairly brisk, tightly scheduled sequence of stellar generations. A universe tens of trillions of years old removes that time pressure entirely — there's ample time for the observed abundance to accumulate at a far more leisurely pace, which the book treats as a point in the old-age model's favor rather than a coincidence needing its own separate explanation.

Where are the first-generation stars?

Standard cosmology predicts that the universe's very first stars — metal-free, built purely from primordial hydrogen and helium — should still be detectable somewhere, and none has ever been conclusively found. On the old-universe reading, that's not a problem with early star-formation theory at all: with tens of trillions of years having passed, every star from that original, metal-free population has long since burned out or been chemically enriched by earlier generations of supernovae, leaving nothing pristine left to find.

From formula to a direct data comparison

Testing it: Type Ia supernova luminosities

Type Ia supernovae are one of cosmology's standard candles: they're thought to detonate at a consistent mass — the Chandrasekhar limit — and so should reach a fairly consistent peak brightness, which lets astronomers infer distance from how bright one looks. That consistency gives the book a direct test: recompute the implied distance to a large sample of supernovae under each of the two distance-redshift relationships, and see which produces more physically sensible brightness values.

Filtering a sample of 8,830 Type Ia supernovae down to a usable set, the book finds their average peak magnitude comes out to −18.851 under standard GR distances — dimmer than the theoretical minimum brightness of a single white dwarf detonation, around −19.3, which is a physical impossibility for an event that's supposed to be one. Recomputed under this chapter's entropic distances, the same sample averages −21.2627, comfortably brighter than that floor. More strikingly, 46.42% of the sample comes out "impossible" — dimmer than the theoretical minimum — under GR distances, versus only about 2% under entropic distances.

Plotted directly, distance against apparent magnitude, the entropic-distance data sits tightly along the inverse-square law's expected line across the whole sample. The GR-distance data tracks it well at low redshift but drifts increasingly off it beyond about z ≈ 0.2, opening a gap of roughly 7 billion light-years between where the two trendlines predict a magnitude-25 object should sit.

Four checks against the oldest things we can actually measure

Testing it: white dwarfs, black dwarfs, and two lone objects

White dwarfs: an age nothing exceeds

White dwarfs cool at a well-understood, predictable rate, which makes their measured temperature a direct age clock. Filtering the Montreal White Dwarf Database down to 33,851 entries above the hydrogen-fusion mass floor of 0.075 solar masses, the book finds that 33,808 of them — 99.87% — are older than the standard model's 13.8-billion-year age for the universe, which is already an uncomfortable number for the standard picture. Against the 42-trillion-year figure derived above, by contrast, zero exceed it.

Two objects sit well apart from the rest of the sample: SDSS J142402.68+061206.5, at 35.4 trillion years, and SDSS J161213.77+163906.3, at 35.7 trillion years — 85% of the full post-decoupling age. Both sit roughly 13 trillion years older than the next-oldest cluster of white dwarfs in the sample, which the book reads as evidence that ordinary star formation lagged well behind decoupling by a substantial margin, rather than as two misclassified outliers to be set aside.

A separate minimum-mass check cuts the other way against the standard model: under a 13.8-billion-year age, only 36% of the white dwarfs in the database are even massive enough to have had time to form at all — implying the other 64% "shouldn't exist" on that timeline. Under the 42-trillion-year age, the minimum mass needed drops to just 0.062 solar masses, and essentially every white dwarf on record is consistent with having had time to form.

Black dwarfs: a near-term falsifiable prediction

A black dwarf is what a white dwarf becomes once it finally finishes cooling to the temperature of its surroundings — invisible in ordinary light, but only if the universe is old enough for any to have formed at all; under the standard 13.8-billion-year age, none should exist anywhere yet. The book catalogues 16 documented candidates: invisible companions inferred from their gravitational effect on a visible partner star in double-star systems, each too massive to be a planet and too cold to be an ordinary white dwarf.

A separate consistency check inside the same database finds that of 27 white dwarfs carrying a binary ("a/b") designation, 24 (89%) have an invisible companion — close to the roughly 90% non-luminous-matter figure calculated earlier in this page for the Milky Way as a whole.

The coldest known neutron star

PSR J2144−3933, at 1.97 ± 0.04 solar masses, has never been detected optically at all — only an upper limit on its temperature, 42,000 K, is known. Standard pulsar-cooling theory predicts a working lifespan of roughly 10 million years; the temperature limit alone implies a minimum age of about 2.945×1012 years, on the order of 3 trillion. As an aside, the book quotes the discovery team's own puzzlement (M. D. Young, 1999) over a different, separately anomalously slow-cooling pulsar — a reminder, on the book's reading, of how incompletely pulsar aging is really understood even inside the standard picture.

The cosmic neutrino background's temperature

The book predicts a cosmic neutrino background temperature of 1.96385 ± 0.00057 K, against standard Big Bang cosmology's own, independently derived prediction of 1.95 K. The book is candid that the near-agreement here is "purely coincidental" — the two models reach their numbers through entirely different mechanisms (electron-positron annihilation heating, versus a simple ratio of neutrino-to-photon decoupling times) — and it says so plainly rather than presenting the match as confirmation of anything. It's a small, honest moment worth noting on its own terms.

Closing speculation, at the edges of an eon

The end and the beginning

This is the second-highest caveat bar on this page, after the entanglement section above. Both pieces below reach outside mainstream science, and the first leans on ideas — morphic resonance and a reincarnation-like continuity between cosmic eons — that this site's Conscious Set Theory page (Chapter 5) is where the book actually tries to unpack the mechanism, rather than here.

The last observer, and the next eon's first

If a conscious observer's own entanglement is what keeps a region of the universe determinate, what happens once the very last conscious observer in a given cosmic eon dies? The book's answer reaches forward into material this page won't try to fully unpack: it proposes that the last observer of one eon and the first observer of the next are linked by morphic resonance — biologist Rupert Sheldrake's proposed nonlocal tendency for patterns to recur — so that the next eon's starting quantum state isn't arbitrary, but inherited from the one that just ended. The book repeatedly points ahead to where this gets developed properly ("see chapter 5.1," "reincarnation will be discussed in chapter 5.4"), and this page follows that same lead: the puzzle and the proposed link are stated here because they're this chapter's own material, but the mechanism itself is left for the Conscious Set Theory page to cover, the same way Charge Mass Dualism's page leans on Space Particle Dualism's critical photon energy for its W/Z-boson mass estimate rather than re-deriving that energy itself.

A more concrete companion: why the Moon looks the way it does

A second, less extreme piece of speculation closes the chapter with something closer to a testable number. The book proposes that life appears as early as it's geologically possible for it to appear, reasoning that the pre-conscious past of a planet only becomes "real," on this framework's own terms, retroactively — once some later consciousness actually emerges to entangle with it. That reframes the usual "rare Earth" observations — the Moon's apparent size matching the Sun's to about 97.25% precision, Earth's unusually low orbital eccentricity, its magnetic field, a Jupiter-sized debris-sweeper further out in the solar system — not as anthropic selection (we notice these coincidences only because we're here to notice them) but as evidence of the same morphic resonance between eons proposed above: conditions tuned, across cycles, to let consciousness arise as promptly as physically possible each time. It's a more concrete, number-anchored claim than the "last observer" puzzle above, but it rests on exactly the same speculative mechanism.
