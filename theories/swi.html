Elementary Spaces/Theories/Similar-Worlds Interpretation

Theory 1 of 6 · conceived 29 October 2003, Pforzheim, Germany

We exist in every world we can't tell apart

The book's founding idea reframes superposition: not one particle in many places, but one consciousness sitting inside every version of the universe that currently looks the same. This page walks through how that idea is argued for, how it stacks up against the other interpretations of quantum mechanics, and the experiment the book proposes to actually test it.

Where the theory starts

A cloud of consciousness in phase space

Quantum mechanics has a well-known limit on precision: a particle's position and its momentum can never both be pinned down exactly. Squeeze one down and the other spreads out. That's Heisenberg's uncertainty principle — — and the strange part isn't that we can't measure both at once. It's that the particle doesn't seem to have both properties at once, independent of anyone measuring them.

The same strangeness shows up in the double-slit experiment: a single particle sent toward two open slits builds up an interference pattern, as if it travelled through both slits and interfered with itself. It doesn't settle into one path until the moment something registers which path it took. Before that, most physicists agree it's meaningless to ask which slit it "really" went through.

Standard quantum theory calls this a superposition — a particle in many states at once, until a "measurement" resolves it into one. But what actually counts as a measurement was never settled. A single particle hitting another particle doesn't know anything. A measuring device is macroscopic, but it doesn't know what it's measuring either, until a person reads the result and understands what it means. That thread — that only knowing something, which requires a conscious mind, can end a superposition — is what historically tied quantum mechanics to consciousness at all, in the interpretation Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg first proposed and John von Neumann and Eugene Wigner later made explicit.

Everett's answer, and a different one

In 1957 Hugh Everett proposed a way to avoid giving consciousness any special role: the many-worlds interpretation. On his view, superposition never stops — it's just that once it reaches human scale, the different outcomes split into separate, non-interfering worlds, and each copy of the observer only ever sees their own branch. It's popular because it keeps quantum mechanics purely mechanical. But the book raises a specific objection: if consciousness is doing nothing in this picture — if nothing is really being chosen — then why does it feel like something is happening? Why does each branch-copy feel a flow of time at all, rather than simply existing, inertly, the way a point on a block-universe diagram does?

The Similar-Worlds Interpretation takes the opposite route. Picture every possible state of the entire universe as a single point in an enormous space of possibilities — physicists call this a phase space. Nearby points are near-identical universes. In an ordinary classical picture, the passage of time is just a point drifting through that space. The book's proposal is to replace that single point with a small cloud: our consciousness sits inside every point in the cloud — every version of the universe that's currently indistinguishable from every other — all at once. Superposition is what that cloud looks like from the inside. A measurement doesn't choose a hidden value out of nowhere; it just shrinks the cloud down to the states still consistent with what was observed.

What that buys back: entanglement without spooky action

This picture has an immediate payoff for one of quantum mechanics' oldest puzzles, the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox. Two entangled particles seem to coordinate their properties — say, opposite spins — no matter how far apart they are, instantaneously, which looks like it should violate relativity's speed limit. In the similar-worlds picture there's no coordination to explain: because every point in the cloud is a state of the whole universe, the two particles simply already have opposite spins in every one of the shared worlds. Nothing has to travel between them for that agreement to hold, any more than two socks from the same pair "communicate" to be a matching set. The book calls this the fuzzy observer principle.

A relativistic complication

Why the theory gives up on "spacetime" as one thing

Relativity complicates the phase-space picture, because relativity denies that there's one universal "now" — simultaneity depends on how fast you're moving. So which slice of the universe is the cloud of consciousness supposed to be a cloud of?

The book's answer is to stop treating spacetime as a single real object and instead assign reality only to each conscious observer's past light cone — everything whose light has had time to reach them. A remote alien on our "now-slice" could, in principle, drift into a reference frame where our future already lies on their now-slice — but that doesn't make our future real, because that alien still can't see it; they can only ever see their own past light cone. Nothing our future can influence has happened yet, so nothing has forced it into a definite state. It's genuinely still open, the way quantum mechanics says it has to be.

That raises an obvious worry, which the book calls Wigner's conflict: if only what's been measured is real, are other people around you unreal until you look at them? The proposed way out is entanglement — it links different observers' wavefunctions together so they change in a synchronized way, without needing a single shared spacetime to do it. Wigner himself ran into this exact puzzle in his own version of a consciousness-based interpretation and, notably, never resolved it to his satisfaction.

The upshot, on the book's account, is that "spacetime" was never one thing to begin with — space and time can influence each other without being fused into a single entity, and the past isn't a place that still physically exists somewhere. This move matters beyond this chapter: it's also what the book uses later to argue that wormhole-style time travel can't be built, and it sets up Space Particle Dualism's account of what's actually doing the bending that general relativity attributes to curved spacetime.

The argument for a non-mechanical mind

Why consciousness, and not just computation

If something has to end a superposition, and it isn't just "size" or "scale" doing it, why does it have to be consciousness specifically, rather than any sufficiently complex physical process? The book's argument runs through Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorem: any formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove from within itself. One consequence, on the book's reading, is that logical reasoning can't be fully automated — a "robot mathematician" that substitutes for a human is impossible in principle, because genuine reasoning needs access to meaning and truth, not just rule-following. That gives a working definition: consciousness is an incomputable but deterministic process. Deterministic, because the same reasoning given the same input always reaches the same conclusion; incomputable, because no algorithm can simulate it.

That reframes what "quantum randomness" is. Instead of true dice-rolling, the book proposes that outcomes are settled by non-computable determinism — meaningful, free choices made by conscious (and subconscious) minds, which look random from the outside only because we can't predict them the way we predict an algorithm's output.

Two speeds of mind

The book leans on neuroscience — particularly Benjamin Libet's timing experiments — to split this into two levels. A signal takes roughly half a second to become consciously available after it reaches the senses. But the brain also continuously re-samples its environment at about 40 times a second (its "gamma" rhythm), and only about half of that reaches full conscious awareness — which is why the book puts the conscious frame rate at roughly 20 pictures per second against a subconscious rate of 40. One consequence: an ordinary macroscopic object around you is being re-measured so often that it can only stay in superposition for about a fortieth of a second at a time, even though the individual photons and nerve signals reporting on it remain in superposition for the fuller half second before any of that reaches consciousness.

Why psychokinesis, if real, would be so weak

The book connects this timing directly to an old parapsychology puzzle: if minds can influence quantum outcomes, why do lab results (random-number-generator studies averaged across thousands of participants) show only a razor-thin edge over chance — around 51%, not 100%? Its proposed answer isn't "the effect is weak," but that the window to act in is razor-thin: a mind would have to exert a meaningful nudge inside roughly a fortieth of a second, tied to the same gamma rhythm that resets superpositions that quickly. Shifting a large object (macro-PK) is separately hard because it requires coordinating enormous numbers of particles — a large, unlikely drop in entropy — while shifting a single binary outcome (micro-PK) doesn't need to fight entropy at all, just needs to land in that narrow timing window.

How the book positions its own idea

Eight interpretations of quantum mechanics, by the book's own reckoning

The book sorts interpretations along three axes: does the wavefunction ever really change (a "choice" theory) or never (a "no-choice" theory)? Is the cutoff tied to physical scale or to consciousness? And if consciousness is involved, does it just witness the outcome (passive) or actually influence it (active)? The verdicts below are the book's own — not an independent physics assessment.

Copenhagen interpretation Consciousness scale-choice · Bohr, Heisenberg (1927/1957)

Fine as far as it goes — treats the wavefunction as the observer's accessible knowledge — but only because it never asks what happens once the observer's own brain is included in the system. Extending it to cover the observer is what produces the next interpretation.

Von Neumann–Wigner (orthodox) interpretation Passive consciousness choice · von Neumann, Wigner, Wheeler

"Correct but incomplete," in the book's words: it's the Copenhagen view applied to the whole universe including the observer, and the book treats the quantum-eraser result as direct evidence for it over any scale-dependent alternative. It just doesn't yet say whether consciousness only witnesses the collapse or actually steers it.

Interactionalism Active consciousness choice · Eccles, Radin, Stapp

The book's preferred predecessor — the version of Von Neumann–Wigner where consciousness doesn't just watch the collapse but has some influence on its outcome. Called "the most correct pre-existing view," mainly lacking a relativistic treatment and a neuroscience-grounded account of timing.

Decoherence / many-minds Passive, no-choice · H. Dieter Zeh (1970)

Rejected. On the book's argument it treats measurement as a loss rather than a gain of information, has no principled account of the flow of time, and depends on an undefined "wavefunction of the universe" that dissolves once any of its supposedly fixed parameters turn out to vary.

Many-worlds interpretation Relativistic scale, no-choice · Hugh Everett (1957)

Credited with inspiring parts of the book's own approach, but judged to only relocate the measurement problem rather than solve it — it still needs an arbitrary scale at which worlds "split," and the book holds that quantum-eraser results and superconductivity rule out any scale-dependent cutoff.

Many-worlds immortality theory Active, no-choice · Moravec, Marchal, Tegmark

The idea that consciousness always finds a branch where it survives. The book calls this self-contradictory — it would grant every observer effectively unlimited psychokinetic power over their own fate — and, like standard many-worlds, considers it ruled out by the quantum-eraser result.

Similar-Worlds Interpretation Relativistic consciousness choice · Sky Darmos (2003) — this page

The book's own proposal: a relativistic extension of Von Neumann–Wigner built on the phase-space "cloud" picture above, later joined to Space Particle Dualism to explain why probabilities come out wave-shaped in the first place.

Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) Active scale-choice · Roger Penrose (1989), Stuart Hameroff

Treated with real respect but ultimately turned down: collapse is triggered by a gravitational threshold rather than by consciousness, which the book argues explains away consciousness rather than accounting for it, and requires an unrealistic degree of quantum isolation inside brain microtubules.

From interpretation to experiment

Testing it: the quantum-eraser proposal

Most interpretations of quantum mechanics are usually treated as philosophical add-ons that don't change any prediction. The book disagrees, and points to the quantum eraser as the reason why.

The idea builds on John Archibald Wheeler's delayed-choice experiments from the 1970s–80s, which showed that whether a photon "behaves" as a wave or a particle isn't decided until the measurement choice is made — even if that choice comes after the photon was emitted. Wheeler put it bluntly:

"To be is to be perceived."

John Archibald Wheeler, paraphrasing Bishop Berkeley

The quantum eraser pushes this further. Two entangled photons are created together. One is sent toward a double slit with polarizers marking which slit it passed through — which destroys the interference pattern, as marking always does. Its entangled partner is sent to a separate detector. If that partner photon is then passed through a third polarizer that undoes the marking, the interference pattern reappears — and this erasure can happen after the first photon has already hit the screen. That means the screen, despite being macroscopic, was still in superposition. It hadn't "measured" anything yet, in the sense that matters.

So somewhere between the screen and the observer's consciousness, the superposition does eventually collapse. The book's proposal is to find exactly where, by delaying the erasing decision longer and longer — using the entangled partner's travel time as a clock — while human observers, animals, or even robots sit at varying distances from the experiment. If erasing stops working once a signal would have reached a given observer's brain, that observer's consciousness is implicated in the collapse. Push the delay past where a robot's sensors register the result, and if erasing still works, the robot isn't conscious in the sense this experiment tests for.

How you'd actually delay a photon by half a second

Photons are fast: in half a second, light in a vacuum covers about 150,000 km, and even slowed inside optical fiber it's still around 105,000 km — far too long a path for a tabletop setup. The book walks through three ways to buy that delay without ever "measuring" the photon in transit:

Fiber-optic loop. Route the entangled partner into a glass-fiber loop it can circulate inside indefinitely, with an entry point that can later be switched into an exit pointing at the third polarizer. Glass fiber's shallow reflection angles avoid the photon-loss problem that ordinary or even high-end "supermirror" cavities run into.

Slowing light with a Bose–Einstein condensate. Using electromagnetically induced transparency, physicist Lene Hau's group slowed light by a factor of about 30 million inside an ultra-cold sodium condensate — enough, the book calculates, that a half-second delay would need a chamber only around 5 meters across.

Storing light in a crystal. A 2013 German team used the same technique to absorb light into a crystal and hold it there — with a control beam preventing re-emission — for delays of up to a full minute, far more than the experiment needs.

The whole setup, the book notes, would double as the first real version of Schrödinger's cat: an animal (or a robot rigged to self-destruct on "seeing" an interference pattern) sealed inside a Faraday cage, with the eraser deciding — after the cage is opened — whether it had been in superposition the whole time.

Status: proposed, not yet run. As of the book's most recent update, this experiment hasn't been performed. The book notes that Dean Radin expressed interest in attempting a version of it in his own lab, but no results exist to report.

Where the evidence gets contested

Nonlocal perturbation and psychokinesis

Everything in this section rests on experimental claims from parapsychology — chiefly Dean Radin's — that mainstream physics does not treat as established, and whose correct interpretation is disputed even between the parapsychologists involved, as described below. It's included because the book leans on it heavily, not as independent confirmation of anything.

In 2016, Radin presented a double-slit experiment he titled "Experimental test of the Von Neumann–Wigner interpretation." Participants didn't literally look at the slits — if they had, the photons would have ended up in their eyes rather than at the detector. Instead they focused on the slits mentally, in what Radin describes as a remote-viewing, or "inner eye," mode. The claimed result: the interferometric visibility of the pattern shifted measurably, at a reported five-sigma significance.

Here the book and Radin part ways on interpretation, and the book is candid about the disagreement. Radin frames it as the observers remote-viewing the photons' paths — a form of perception. The book argues that doesn't quite make sense, because a photon mid-flight through a double slit doesn't yet have a definite position to "view" — there's no existing information there to perceive. Its preferred reading is that participants performed ordinary micro-psychokinesis, nudging an already-superposed outcome rather than perceiving a hidden one.

The same fork shows up in a separate line of experiments: random-number-generator recordings that sit unopened and un-observed for hours, days, or (in one case) half a year, where participants can apparently still influence the outcome after the fact. Radin and fellow researcher Stephan Schwartz read this as evidence of retrocausality — influence running backward in time from the later observation to the earlier recording. The book instead argues the record was simply still in superposition the entire time it went unobserved, so there's no need to invoke backward causation at all — ordinary psychokinesis, just exercised on a delay.

The book is explicit about what this evidence can and can't settle. It can't determine when or where a wavefunction collapses — that's what the quantum-eraser experiment above is for. All it argues for is that some observer influence on quantum outcomes exists at all, which is a much narrower claim, and even that claim depends entirely on accepting Radin's experimental results as this section describes them.

A different account of the brain's clock

The rate of wavefunction collapse

Penrose and Hameroff's Orchestrated Objective Reduction model makes a specific, checkable claim: the brain's gamma waves, oscillating at 40 Hz, correspond to a gravity-triggered collapse rate. The book proposes a plainer alternative — that 40 Hz is just the ordinary firing rate of the neurons doing the measuring, with no gravity required.

It offers a small, testable-feeling piece of supporting arithmetic. If the subconscious samples the world 40 times a second, a person walking at an ordinary pace five meters away moves about 35mm between samples. At arm's length — roughly 0.7 meters, the book's assumed "engaging distance" — that corresponds to an angular size of about 0.05mm, strikingly close to the 0.058–0.072mm diameter of the smallest objects visible to the naked eye. The suggested reading: our sampling rate is tuned to just barely resolve the smallest details we can see moving at everyday walking speed, which is a much simpler story than deriving the same number from graviton emission thresholds.
