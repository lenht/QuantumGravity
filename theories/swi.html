Elementary Spaces/Theories/Similar-Worlds Interpretation

Theory 1 of 6 · conceived 29 October 2003, Pforzheim, Germany

We exist in every world we can't tell apart

Darmos's founding idea gives quantum mechanics an intuitive picture of superposition: not one particle in many places, but one consciousness sitting inside every version of the universe that currently looks the same. It's a bold attempt to answer a question physics has never settled — what a measurement is — and it comes with a concrete experiment to test it. This page follows his reasoning, shows how it relates to the other major interpretations, and explains the proposed test.

Where the theory starts

A cloud of consciousness in phase space

Quantum mechanics has a famous limit on precision: a particle's position and momentum can never both be pinned down exactly. Squeeze one and the other spreads out. That's Heisenberg's uncertainty principle — — and the deep puzzle isn't that we can't measure both at once. It's that the particle doesn't seem to have both properties at once, whether or not anyone is measuring.

The double-slit experiment shows the same strangeness. A single particle sent toward two open slits builds up an interference pattern, as if it passed through both. It doesn't settle on one path until something registers which, and before that, most physicists agree, asking which slit it "really" used has no meaning.

Standard theory calls this a superposition — a particle in many states at once until a "measurement" resolves it. What counts as a measurement has never been agreed on. A particle striking another particle knows nothing. A detector is macroscopic, but doesn't know what it measured until a person reads and understands the result. The thought that only knowing something, which takes a conscious mind, ends a superposition is what historically tied quantum mechanics to consciousness — in the interpretation Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg first proposed and John von Neumann and Eugene Wigner later made explicit. Darmos takes that respected tradition seriously and asks what it looks like pushed all the way.

Everett's answer, and a different one

In 1957 Hugh Everett proposed a way to avoid giving consciousness any special role: the many-worlds interpretation. Superposition never stops; at human scale the outcomes split into separate, non-interfering worlds, and each copy of the observer sees only its own branch. It's popular because it keeps quantum mechanics purely mechanical. Darmos objects: if nothing is really chosen, why does anything feel like it's happening? Why does each branch-copy experience a flow of time at all, rather than simply existing, inertly, like a point on a block-universe diagram?

The Similar-Worlds Interpretation takes the other route. Picture every possible state of the entire universe as a single point in an enormous space of possibilities — phase space. Nearby points are near-identical universes. Classically, the passage of time is one point drifting through that space. Darmos's proposal replaces the single point with a small cloud: our consciousness sits inside every point of the cloud — every version of the universe currently indistinguishable from the others — all at once. Superposition is simply what that cloud looks like from the inside. A measurement doesn't pull a hidden value out of nowhere; it shrinks the cloud to the states consistent with what was observed.

What that buys back: entanglement without spooky action

This picture pays off immediately on one of quantum mechanics' oldest puzzles, the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox. Two entangled particles seem to coordinate their properties — say, opposite spins — instantaneously across any distance, which looks like it breaks relativity's speed limit. In the similar-worlds picture there's nothing to coordinate: every point in the cloud is a state of the whole universe, so the two particles already have opposite spins in every shared world. Nothing travels between them, any more than two socks from one pair "communicate" to be a match. Darmos calls this the fuzzy observer principle.

A relativistic complication

Why the theory gives up on "spacetime" as one thing

Relativity challenges any phase-space picture, because it denies a single universal "now" — simultaneity depends on how fast you're moving. So which slice of the universe is the cloud a cloud of? Darmos treats this as a problem to solve.

His answer is to stop treating spacetime as one real object and assign reality only to each conscious observer's past light cone — everything whose light has had time to reach them. A remote alien on our "now-slice" might drift into a frame where our future lies on their now-slice, but that doesn't make our future real: the alien still can't see it. Nothing our future can influence has happened yet, so nothing has forced it into a definite state. It's genuinely open, as quantum mechanics says it must be.

That raises a worry Darmos names Wigner's conflict: if only what's been measured is real, are other people unreal until you look at them? His way out is entanglement, which links different observers' wavefunctions so they change in step without one shared spacetime. Wigner himself met this exact puzzle in his own consciousness-based interpretation and never resolved it to his satisfaction — so Darmos is picking up a genuinely hard thread.

The upshot is that "spacetime" was never one thing: space and time can influence each other without being fused into one entity, and the past isn't a place that still physically exists. This move echoes through the framework. Darmos later uses it to argue that wormhole-style time travel can't be built, and it sets up Space Particle Dualism's account of what is actually doing the bending general relativity attributes to curved spacetime.

The argument for a non-mechanical mind

Why consciousness, and not just computation

If something has to end a superposition, and it isn't just scale, why consciousness rather than any sufficiently complex physical process? Darmos's argument runs through Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorem: any formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic contains true statements it can't prove from within. On his reading, logical reasoning can't be fully automated — a "robot mathematician" is impossible in principle, because real reasoning needs access to meaning and truth, not just rule-following. This is an argument of the Lucas–Penrose kind, which many logicians and philosophers dispute, so it's best read as Darmos's starting premise — shared with Roger Penrose, whom he cites as his primary influence — rather than a settled result. From it comes a working definition: consciousness is an incomputable but deterministic process. Deterministic, because the same reasoning on the same input reaches the same conclusion; incomputable, because no algorithm can simulate it.

That reframes quantum randomness. Rather than true dice-rolling, Darmos proposes that outcomes are settled by non-computable determinism — meaningful, free choices by conscious (and subconscious) minds, which look random from outside only because we can't predict them as we predict an algorithm's output. It's an attempt to restore both free will and lawfulness.

Two speeds of mind

Darmos grounds this in neuroscience, particularly Benjamin Libet's timing experiments, to distinguish two levels. A signal takes roughly half a second to become consciously available after it reaches the senses. But the brain also re-samples its environment about 40 times a second (its "gamma" rhythm), and only about half of that reaches full awareness — so Darmos puts the conscious frame rate near 20 per second against a subconscious 40. One consequence: an ordinary macroscopic object around you is re-measured so often that it can stay in superposition only about a fortieth of a second at a time, even though the photons and nerve signals reporting on it stay in superposition for the full half second before reaching consciousness.

Why psychokinesis, if real, would be so weak

Darmos connects this timing to an old parapsychology puzzle: if minds can influence quantum outcomes, why do lab results (random-number-generator studies averaged over thousands of participants) show only a razor-thin edge over chance, around 51% rather than 100%? His answer isn't that the effect is weak, but that the window to act in is very narrow: a mind would have to nudge the outcome inside roughly a fortieth of a second, tied to the same gamma rhythm that resets superpositions so quickly. Moving a large object (macro-PK) is separately hard because it means coordinating enormous numbers of particles — a large, unlikely drop in entropy — while nudging a single binary outcome (micro-PK) needs no such fight, just the right timing. One timing argument thus accounts for both the weakness and the shape of the reported effect. As with the rest of this material, the underlying experimental results remain contested (see the nonlocal-perturbation section).

How Darmos positions his own idea

Eight interpretations of quantum mechanics, by Darmos's own reckoning

Darmos sorts interpretations along three axes: does the wavefunction ever really change (a "choice" theory) or never (a "no-choice" theory)? Is the cutoff tied to physical scale or to consciousness? And if consciousness is involved, does it merely witness the outcome (passive) or influence it (active)? This scheme makes the landscape easier to navigate. The verdicts below are Darmos's own, offered as a guide to his reasoning rather than an independent physics assessment.

Copenhagen interpretation Consciousness scale-choice · Bohr, Heisenberg (1927/1957)

Sound as far as it goes, in Darmos's view: it treats the wavefunction as the observer's accessible knowledge. It doesn't ask what happens once the observer's own brain is included in the system — extending it to do so leads to the next interpretation.

Von Neumann–Wigner (orthodox) interpretation Passive consciousness choice · von Neumann, Wigner, Wheeler

"Correct but incomplete," in Darmos's words: it's the Copenhagen view applied to the whole universe including the observer, and Darmos treats the quantum-eraser result as direct evidence for it over any scale-dependent alternative. It leaves open whether consciousness only witnesses collapse or actually steers it.

Interactionalism Active consciousness choice · Eccles, Radin, Stapp

Darmos's closest predecessor — the version of Von Neumann–Wigner in which consciousness influences the outcome. He calls it "the most correct pre-existing view," lacking mainly a relativistic treatment and a neuroscience-grounded account of timing, the gaps he sets out to fill.

Decoherence / many-minds Passive, no-choice · H. Dieter Zeh (1970)

Set aside as a complete account. In Darmos's view it treats measurement as a loss rather than a gain of information, offers no principled account of the flow of time, and relies on an undefined "wavefunction of the universe." Decoherence as a physical process is well established in mainstream physics; his objection is to it serving as a full interpretation of measurement.

Many-worlds interpretation Relativistic scale, no-choice · Hugh Everett (1957)

Darmos credits it with inspiring parts of his own approach, but feels it only relocates the measurement problem: it still needs a scale at which worlds "split," and he holds that quantum-eraser results and superconductivity rule out any scale-dependent cutoff. Many-worlds remains among the most widely discussed interpretations, and its supporters answer these objections differently.

Many-worlds immortality theory Active, no-choice · Moravec, Marchal, Tegmark

The idea that consciousness always finds a branch where it survives. Darmos finds it self-contradictory — it would give every observer effectively unlimited psychokinetic power over their own fate — and, like standard many-worlds, considers it ruled out by the quantum-eraser result.

Similar-Worlds Interpretation Relativistic consciousness choice · Sky Darmos (2003) — this page

Darmos's own proposal: a relativistic extension of Von Neumann–Wigner built on the phase-space "cloud" picture, later joined to Space Particle Dualism to explain why probabilities come out wave-shaped.

Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) Active scale-choice · Roger Penrose (1989), Stuart Hameroff

Treated with respect but set aside: collapse is triggered by a gravitational threshold rather than by consciousness, which Darmos argues explains consciousness away rather than accounting for it, and he finds the quantum isolation it needs inside brain microtubules unrealistic.

From interpretation to experiment

Testing it: the quantum-eraser proposal

Interpretations of quantum mechanics are usually treated as philosophical add-ons that change no prediction. Darmos disagrees, and this proposal is where the theory earns its place as physics: it points to a concrete experiment that could tell interpretations apart.

The idea builds on John Archibald Wheeler's delayed-choice experiments of the 1970s–80s, which showed that whether a photon "behaves" as a wave or a particle isn't decided until the measurement choice is made — even after the photon was emitted. Wheeler put it bluntly:

"To be is to be perceived."

John Archibald Wheeler, paraphrasing Bishop Berkeley

The quantum eraser pushes this further. Two entangled photons are created together. One is sent toward a double slit with polarizers marking which slit it passed through, which destroys the interference pattern, as marking always does. Its partner goes to a separate detector. If the partner is then passed through a third polarizer that undoes the marking, the interference pattern reappears — and this erasure can happen after the first photon has hit the screen. So the screen, though macroscopic, was still in superposition; it hadn't "measured" anything in the sense that matters. (That is Darmos's reading. Many physicists see delayed-choice eraser results as fully accounted for by standard quantum mechanics, with no special role for consciousness — which is part of why he proposes the extended tests below.)

So somewhere between the screen and the observer's consciousness, the superposition does collapse. Darmos proposes finding where, by delaying the erasing decision longer and longer — using the entangled partner's travel time as a clock — while human observers, animals, or even robots sit at varying distances from the experiment. If erasing stops working once a signal would have reached a given observer's brain, that observer's consciousness is implicated in the collapse. Push the delay past where a robot's sensors register the result, and if erasing still works, the robot isn't conscious in the sense this experiment tests for. Few interpretations offer a test this direct.

How you'd actually delay a photon by half a second

In half a second, light in a vacuum covers about 150,000 km, and even in optical fiber around 105,000 km — far too long a path for a tabletop setup. Darmos works through three ways to buy that delay without "measuring" the photon in transit:

Fiber-optic loop. Route the entangled partner into a glass-fiber loop it can circulate inside indefinitely, with an entry point that can later be switched into an exit pointing at the third polarizer. Glass fiber's shallow reflection angles avoid the photon loss that ordinary or even "supermirror" cavities run into.

Slowing light with a Bose–Einstein condensate. Using electromagnetically induced transparency, Lene Hau's group slowed light by a factor of about 30 million inside an ultra-cold sodium condensate — enough, by Darmos's calculation, that a half-second delay would need a chamber only around 5 meters across.

Storing light in a crystal. A 2013 German team used the same technique to absorb light into a crystal and hold it there — a control beam preventing re-emission — for up to a full minute, far more than the experiment needs.

Darmos notes the setup would double as the first real Schrödinger's cat: an animal (or a robot rigged to self-destruct on "seeing" an interference pattern) sealed inside a Faraday cage, with the eraser deciding — after the cage is opened — whether it had been in superposition the whole time.

Status: proposed, not yet run. As of Darmos's most recent update this experiment hasn't been performed. He notes that Dean Radin expressed interest in attempting a version in his own lab, but no results exist. The proposal stands as a well-specified invitation to test.

Where the evidence gets contested

Nonlocal perturbation and psychokinesis

This section draws on experimental claims from parapsychology — chiefly Dean Radin's — that mainstream physics does not treat as established, and that even the researchers involved interpret differently, as described below. Darmos leans on them as supporting evidence, and they're presented here on his terms; the fair reading is that they're suggestive to him and open questions to most other physicists.

In 2016, Radin presented a double-slit experiment titled "Experimental test of the Von Neumann–Wigner interpretation." Participants didn't literally look at the slits — the photons would have ended up in their eyes rather than at the detector. Instead they focused on the slits mentally, in what Radin describes as a remote-viewing, or "inner eye," mode. The claimed result: the interferometric visibility of the pattern shifted measurably, at a reported five-sigma significance.

Here Darmos parts ways with Radin on interpretation, and he's candid about it. Radin frames it as observers remote-viewing the photons' paths — a form of perception. Darmos argues that doesn't fit, because a photon mid-flight through a double slit has no definite position yet to "view" — there's no existing information to perceive. His preferred reading is that participants performed ordinary micro-psychokinesis, nudging an already-superposed outcome rather than perceiving a hidden one.

The same fork appears in a separate line of experiments: random-number-generator recordings left unopened and unobserved for hours, days, or (in one case) half a year, where participants can apparently still influence the outcome afterward. Radin and fellow researcher Stephan Schwartz read this as retrocausality — influence running backward from the later observation to the earlier recording. Darmos offers a more economical reading: the record was still in superposition the whole time it went unobserved, so no backward causation is needed — ordinary psychokinesis, exercised on a delay. His framework reduces the number of exotic ingredients another interpretation requires.

Darmos is explicit about what this evidence can and can't settle. It can't determine when or where a wavefunction collapses — that's what the quantum-eraser experiment is for. It argues only that some observer influence on quantum outcomes exists, a much narrower claim, and one that depends on accepting Radin's results as described here.

A different account of the brain's clock

The rate of wavefunction collapse

Penrose and Hameroff's Orchestrated Objective Reduction model makes a checkable claim: the brain's 40 Hz gamma waves correspond to a gravity-triggered collapse rate. Darmos proposes a plainer alternative — 40 Hz is simply the ordinary firing rate of the neurons doing the measuring, with no gravity required.

He supports it with arithmetic anyone can check. If the subconscious samples the world 40 times a second, a person walking at an ordinary pace five meters away moves about 35 mm between samples. At arm's length — roughly 0.7 meters, Darmos's assumed "engaging distance" — that corresponds to an angular size of about 0.05 mm, strikingly close to the 0.058–0.072 mm diameter of the smallest objects visible to the naked eye. The suggested reading: our sampling rate is tuned to just resolve the smallest details we can see moving at walking speed — a simpler story than deriving the same number from graviton emission thresholds.
