Elementary Spaces/Theories/Conscious Set Theory

Theory 5 of 6 · conceived 6 October 2004, extended through 25 May 2026

A feeling is a set, not a substance

Space Particle Dualism and the Similar-Worlds Interpretation both lean on consciousness as load-bearing without pinning down what it is. Here Darmos takes that on directly, with a genuinely elegant move: borrowing Gottlob Frege's trick for defining numbers, he defines consciousness itself as a set — not a substance in the brain, but the collection of every relevant experience anyone has had of something. It's the most ambitious chapter of the framework, because it asks the question physics usually avoids. From that idea he builds, across two decades of revision, nine preconditions for consciousness, a physics-flavored taxonomy of psychokinesis, an account of near-death experience and reincarnation, and — in the most far-reaching material on this site — claims of contact with the deceased and a proposed path toward faster-than-light communication. This page follows the whole arc and says, as it goes, how much rests on tested ground and how much doesn't.

Where the theory starts

Consciousness as a Frege-style set

Gottlob Frege defined a number like "three" as the set of every group of three things that exists — an alternative to Georg Cantor's construction from nested sets of the empty set. Conscious Set Theory applies Frege's move to qualia: the feeling of red isn't a signal stored in one person's visual cortex, it's the set of every experience of red anyone has ever had. Two people's experiences of red feel alike for the same reason two groups of three apples are both "three": they draw on the same underlying set rather than each manufacturing a private copy. It's a clean answer to a question that has resisted most attempts, namely why one person's red should feel like another's.

Four worlds

Darmos extends Karl Popper's three "worlds" (physical, mathematical/abstract, mental) with Popper's later fourth, the world of culture: world-1, physical objects and particles; world-2, the relations between world-1 objects (a thermostat's wiring, a camera's optics); world-3, symbolic or representational relations, which a camera or book has plenty of but which carry no Qualia on their own; and world-4, the "world of collective experience" — close to Popper's world of culture, but not dependent on a surviving physical record. Burning every copy of a book erases its content from Popper's world-3; on this account it doesn't touch world-4, because that content was never stored there physically.

Four kinds of relation

A second distinction, used repeatedly later: type-1 relations hold between physical bodies in the present moment; type-2, between one person's own thoughts; type-3, between a handful of people's thoughts — rare, and the basis of ordinary telepathy claims; and type-4, across everyone's experience at once — the basis of Qualia and, on Darmos's account, of mathematical truth, which he treats as continuous with Qualia.

Why must consciousness be non-algorithmic rather than merely complex? Darmos's answer runs through Kurt Gödel's 1930 incompleteness theorem: any formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic contains truths it can't prove from within, so an algorithm alone can never reach genuine understanding or a felt flow of time. This is the same "incomputable but deterministic" definition introduced on the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page; this chapter reuses it and builds on top.

A brief aesthetics aside: Darmos also explains why experiences feel good or bad in these terms — harmony as unusually rich internal relations within a set, pain as a signal kept deliberately poor in relations so it can't be mistaken for anything else, and humor or paradox as pleasurable because a "reductio ad absurdum" acts as an anti-relation, connecting two things by showing they can't be connected. It's a minor thread, not load-bearing.

What has to be true for something to be conscious

Nine preconditions for consciousness

Darmos turns the Frege-style definition into a checklist: nine conditions a physical system must meet to take part in a Qualia-bearing set. Each cleanly excludes some class of system that clearly isn't conscious — a practical way of making an abstract idea something you can apply.

  1. Information processing — excludes plants.
  2. Quantum coherence — excludes ordinary computers.
  3. Large entangled quantum superpositions, or holism — rules out Objective Reduction theories that collapse the wavefunction locally rather than across the whole brain.
  4. Memory — excludes certain fish.
  5. A non-algorithmic response pattern — excludes insects and computers.
  6. Sensitivity of information processing to microscopic quantum states — the opposite of the resilience good engineering aims for, which is why it excludes computers.
  7. An inventory of already-useful algorithms, built up through Darwinian and Lamarckian evolution — Darmos adds, speculatively, that "some guidance through collective consciousness based upon the evolution in former universes is also conceivable" — excludes plants.
  8. Pre-conditioned algorithms for emotional reactions, tied directly to Qualia — excludes insects.
  9. An information filter separating conscious from subconscious processing — excludes computers.

Two conditions connect to material already on this site. Condition 3 is where Darmos takes a position on Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff's Orchestrated Objective Reduction: he credits their microtubule research as useful — superposition-preserving structures that could enlarge coherence across the brain — while setting aside their collapse mechanism: "Hameroff's microtubule theory is good, but it does in no way require OR as a basis." That's consistent with the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's verdict on Orch-OR. And the timing figures behind conditions 2, 5, and 6 reuse numbers established there: signals stay in superposition for roughly half a second, while the brain's overall coherent state resolves about 20 times a second — the same 20 Hz conscious / 40 Hz subconscious split introduced on SWI's page, not a new estimate.

To test whether another system meets these conditions, Darmos points back to the tool proposed for this purpose elsewhere on this site: watch for a roughly half-second conscious reaction time distinct from reflex speed, or run the quantum-eraser test described on SWI's page against the candidate directly, rather than inferring consciousness from behavior.

Two anecdotes for "non-algorithmic response pattern"

Darmos illustrates condition 5 with two of his own observations, offered as illustrations rather than evidence: a bee trapped in a van that repeatedly tried a closed window instead of the open door beside it, and a worm that, blocked by a small obstacle, took the same wrong turn nine times before trying the other direction. Both are read as showing that insects and simple invertebrates run a fixed algorithm rather than responding to what's in front of them — but they're first-person observations, not a controlled study.

Where physics leaves room for a nudge

Psychokinesis, thermodynamics, and five levels of intervention

The physics argument here is a sharp one — quantum mechanics only supplies probabilities, never laws, for which outcome occurs — and it's the reasoning already applied to lab-bench psychokinesis on the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page. The named practitioners and their claimed feats are held for the next section, where the caveat gets more specific.

Classically, a shattered teacup reassembling itself on a table is possible — every law of physics permits it — but so astronomically unlikely that it "never happens." Darmos argues an observer nudging a quantum outcome is the same kind of event: quantum mechanics gives probabilities, not laws, so a mind steering the dice slightly violates nothing physical; it only makes an already-unlikely outcome somewhat less rare than chance. What's distinctive here is that he frames the question in thermodynamic terms, which gives it numbers rather than just a story.

How fast could a mind actually push something?

Using the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, Darmos calculates mean thermal particle speeds for nitrogen (470.5 m/s) and iron (337.4 m/s, or 1,214.64 km/h) as the raw material a psychokinetic effect would have to redirect. Mapping Dean Radin's reported 51% average success rate at influencing a quantum random-number generator onto a 0–100% scale — where a 50% chance result is 0% of maximum speed and 100% is 100% — puts a real, reported effect at about 2% of maximum: for iron, 337.4 × 0.02 ≈ 6.748 m/s (24.2928 km/h). That becomes his working "theoretical maximum" psychokinetic shifting speed, reused in the levitation figures below.

How big a drop in entropy does that actually require?

Counting every atom in a one-kilogram object (roughly 1025) and asking how unlikely it is for 2% of them to be nudged toward one direction gives an entropy drop of . But Darmos argues that's the wrong count if the psychokinesis is aimed at something only being watched rather than touched: counting only the neurons doing the perceiving — a visual field "displayed" at 1–7 megapixels, one neuron per pixel — gives a far smaller . His reading: psychokinesis might be easier to exert on something seen through a screen or from a distance than on something touched up close, since only the perceiving neurons need "coordinating," not every atom in the target.

A specific number for the random-number-generator studies

A willed action is modeled as taking about a second to form, then being released into one of the 40 subconscious frames per second already established on this site (the same figure as SWI's 20/40 Hz split), giving , a 51.25% predicted success rate that lines up closely with Radin's roughly 51%. Using the 20 Hz conscious rate instead of the 40 Hz subconscious one gives 52.5%. A related figure for physical dice-tossing, 51.2% per Radin, sits slightly above the RNG number.

From taxonomy to named claims

Practitioners, levitation, and Uri Geller

The names below are specific, real, individually tested people, which is part of what makes this section worth taking seriously rather than treating "psychokinesis research" as a genre. Some were tested by government or skeptic organizations (the Stanford Research Institute, the CIA, the BND, GWUP); others rest only on Darmos's personal correspondence with the practitioner. "Tested by" a named organization is not "independently replicated" or "peer-reviewed," and for most of what follows Darmos's account is the only source. The author's own psi-wheel experiments, described further down, are a first-person, self-reported anecdote rather than lab data — a further notch below even the tested claims — and labeled as such.

Uri Geller

Geller was tested by Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff at the Stanford Research Institute and, per Darmos, by the CIA. Physicists David Bohm and John Taylor also investigated him, as, informally, did Richard Feynman, who came away unconvinced but reported no evidence of fraud. Physicist Jack Sarfatti, after a demonstration at Birkbeck College, judged that Geller "demonstrated genuine psychoenergetic ability at Birkbeck... beyond the doubt of any reasonable man." Darmos also reports that roughly two dozen scientific papers concluded Geller was genuine, and that Geller's website lists endorsements from "60 scientists, 32 trick-magicians and 13 celebrities" — Geller's own self-published claim, not independently verified here.

John Taylor is Darmos's cautionary counter-example: initially convinced by Geller, Taylor spent years failing to find an electromagnetic or "fifth force" explanation, and concluded his resulting doubt rested on the lack of an explanation, not on evidence of fraud. Darmos keeps that nuance.

Nina Kulagina

Kulagina (Russia, 1926–1990) was tested by, in Darmos's phrase, "hundreds of scientists, domestic and foreign," over roughly two decades, until her health and her reported ability declined. She often suffered headaches and spinal pain after performing — which Darmos reads as entropy displaced into the practitioner's own body rather than destroyed.

Benedikt Maurer

Maurer (Germany), with two decades of claimed practice, was tested by German intelligence (the BND) and by GWUP, a skeptics' organization, passing a magnet-detection test by correctly identifying which of ten boxes hid a magnet, nine times out of thirteen. Binomial odds by chance: P(X≥9) ≈ 4.906×10-7, roughly one in two million. Darmos adds that GWUP wants to repeat the test, this time with a scan for magnets inside his body, before awarding its prize, with no date set, and that Maurer is the only person to have passed any GWUP test in fifteen years. Maurer himself attributes part of his effects to unusual electromagnetic activity in his own body rather than psychokinesis proper; Darmos treats that as a real, separate, non-nonlocal phenomenon and says so plainly, the same kind of honest confound other pages on this site have pointed out in their sources.

Jean Pierre Girard

Girard (France) has had his metal-bending and object-shifting witnessed by several named physicists and materials scientists — Zbigniew Wolkowski, M. Troublé, Raymond Villetange, C. Bogdanski, and R. D. Mattuck among them — including a repeat demonstration at Utrecht in 1997.

Levitation

Girard's levitation demonstration reached about 0.1 m, implying an initial speed of 1.4 m/s — 20.7% of the 6.748 m/s theoretical maximum above. Maurer claims five lifetime levitation events, only two filmed, one a small hop and one he estimates himself, unverified and not fully released on video, at roughly 0.6 m — implying 3.4 m/s, 50.8% of the maximum. Darmos is explicit that true, sustained levitation — an object held suspended rather than briefly rising and falling — "has not yet been demonstrated under laboratory conditions," a restraint about his own strongest claim in this section.

A personal experiment

The author describes psi-wheel experiments — a balanced aluminum vane on a pivot, of the kind normally shown inside a glass hub or cylinder — claiming a 90–100% success rate, over about half an hour, at making it turn in a direction chosen beforehand, some recorded on video. By his own account these results came even without a glass hub, so the wheel was not isolated from air currents. This is self-reported, not independently tested, and worth holding apart from the Kulagina/Geller/Maurer material above, all of which involved at least one third-party testing organization.

What a Platonic set survives

Conscious sets, near-death experience, and reincarnation

This section reaches further than ordinary philosophy of mind: it extends the Frege-style set model into claims about near-death experience and reincarnation — what, if anything, of a person continues after death, offered in earnest rather than as metaphor. It's also where Darmos works out the mechanism he promised but set aside at the close of Entropic Expansion Cosmology.

A person, on this model, is a Platonic set 𝐍 — the full collection of every conscious experience anyone has had of them. Two useful subsets: 𝐍self, one's own self-experience, and 𝐍watch, everyone currently perceiving them.

Near-death experience

Brain death, on this account, only removes access to 𝐍self — not to 𝐍watch or the full set 𝐍, which keep "evolving" through the perceptions of everyone still observing the person, including the medical staff trying to revive them. Darmos offers this as the mechanism behind reports of near-death patients recalling what doctors and nurses did or said during clinical death, and behind the floating-above-oneself, "enlightenment"-like feeling some survivors describe — read as momentary access to the fuller set 𝐍 rather than just 𝐍self.

Reincarnation

Rather than defining a person by fixed facts — birthplace, name, parentage, all of which can change or prove wrong — Darmos defines a person as "a collection of successive self-similar sets": a temporal chain 𝐍0, 𝐍1, 𝐍2, … A newborn can become the next link in an existing chain if they're the most self-similar continuation available, giving them partial — mostly felt rather than consciously known — access to the prior link's full Platonic set. Darmos calls genuinely checkable cases rare, citing (at footnote weight) a compilation of children's claimed past-life memories reportedly confirmed against real deceased people's biographical details; this page keeps it at that weight rather than building a case study.

The two anecdotes that follow are the author's own first-person experiences — not lab data, not independently witnessed, and not part of his evidentiary case the way the tested practitioners above are. Read them as illustrations of a probability argument, not evidence in their own right.

Two personal anecdotes

A "collective clock effect": waking seconds before an alarm, with no alarm set. Darmos calculates the odds of a random 5-second window lining up with a full day's wake time at 5.78×10-5 (0.0057%) — "should happen only once in 54 years" (though 1 ÷ 5.78×10-5 days works out to about 47 years, not 54) — yet, per the author, it happens often; he illustrates with an anecdote about a friend, called "Haiming," waking at precisely 8:00 am with no alarm.

A synchronicity-in-thought anecdote: the author describes a woman re-contacting him at almost the exact moment he was telling friends a story about her, and calculates the odds of that timing by chance at roughly 7×10-4 (0.07%) — a five-minute window against about seventeen waking hours a day over a week. This page keeps the anecdote generic, without Darmos's specific personal framing, since it concerns a private individual otherwise not part of this story.

A parapsychologist's own field record

Schwartz, the Mobius group, and remote-viewing archaeology

This is the same kind of claim covered elsewhere on this site under the standard contested-evidence caveat for Radin- and Schwartz-style parapsychology research — extended here, not escalated. In Darmos's taxonomy it counts as a type-3 relation: a link between a handful of people's thoughts, not everyone's.

This section covers the biography and case studies of parapsychologist Stephan A. Schwartz and his "Mobius" research group. Field cases of this kind, with named sites and named observers, are among the more concrete things this literature offers.

Project Deep Quest

Psychics Ingo Swann and Hella Hammid independently located a sunken wooden ship near Santa Catalina Island, California — an area where 4,000 prior dives by USC's Institute for Marine and Coastal Studies had found nothing. A robot-arm research submarine confirmed the wreck. The search was monitored by a named neutral observer, Dr. Anne Kahle of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, and oceanographer Don Walsh commented afterward that there was "no way" the team could have known about the wreck by ordinary means.

The Alexandria project (1977–1988)

Eleven psychics located candidate sites; Hella Hammid and George McMullen were sent to three unexpected markings on a map. Results included a water channel matching one documented roughly two hundred years earlier by scientists accompanying Napoleon, whose entrance had since been lost; a site at Nabi Daniel where McMullen, and independently Hammid, each described what they took to be a tomb-like structure they associated with Alexander the Great — further excavation there was blocked (it would have required tearing down part of a mosque), so this was never confirmed either way; and a Byzantine building buried in the Marea desert, where Darmos reports 80% of what the remote viewers described was later confirmed accurate by archaeologists on site. Discoveries credited to the broader project also reportedly include Cleopatra's Palace, Marc Antony's Timonium, and ruins of the Lighthouse of Pharos.

A separate three-day submarine test, deep enough for seawater to shield out extremely-low-frequency radio signals, is described as ruling out ELF transmission as an explanation for the effects.

The "2050 project" (1978–1996)

A long-running survey asked remote-viewers to describe conditions in the year 2050. Darmos's reading is measured rather than triumphant: several apparent hits (AIDS, an "anti-terror war") reflect things that had already begun to emerge by the time of the sessions, even if not yet widely known — so they require nothing beyond ordinary, if nonlocal, present-tense information access. This page keeps that hedge rather than presenting the predictions as confirmed foresight.

Morphic resonance across eons

Rupert Sheldrake's morphic resonance (1981) is introduced here as the overlap between different people's contents within the world of experience — ordinary synchronicity where the overlap is moderate, telepathy where it's very strong. Darmos extends it between cosmic eons: if the past is infinite, as Entropic Expansion Cosmology's periodic cosmology proposes, and morphic resonance is a real physical law rather than confined to genetics, a new eon's starting quantum state could inherit patterns from the eon that just ended — the way, in his analogy, a person doesn't consciously remember a past life but might still "inherit preferences" from one. That is Darmos's answer to the question EEC's closing section raised and declined to resolve: what happens to a cosmic eon once its last conscious observer dies. This page is where that mechanism was always meant to land.

Two examples already on this site

Why physics itself needs the nonlocal realm

Both examples below are already covered, and caveated, elsewhere on this site; this section only uses them to make a narrower philosophical point about where information has to "live." See the linked sections for the physics itself.

Darmos's argument is short: ordinary physics already needs somewhere for information to be "remembered" that isn't a physical trace anyone could point to — what he calls the nonlocal realm (his term for the world of experience, treated as identical with the full wavefunction of the universe).

Black hole entropy. A black hole's precise entropy — which, per Entropic Expansion Cosmology, governs how much the universe expands — can't be read off the horizon from outside. On this account it's remembered, not physically encoded anywhere accessible.

Beam splitters. A photon threading a cascade of beam splitters has to "remember" which path it took in order to arrive only at the correct detector once its path information is checked — the territory of the quantum-eraser mechanics described on SWI's page.

Darmos also gives a brief, respectful nod to a correspondent's competing idea: physicist Steve Dufourny's "pan-codism," which holds that a particle's internal structure encodes its history directly, is described as a more radical mechanist alternative he considers and sets aside — a minor citation, not load-bearing.

Where Darmos's own claims reach furthest

Quantum oracles, and the electronic voice phenomenon

The brain as a "quantum oracle"

Darmos pictures neural pathways forming the way a crystal grows — many possible paths at each branching point, with only those that cascade all the way to an actual conscious result ever "selected," in retrospect. He calls the picture a quantum oracle: an entity that, on his account, can resolve the halting problem. The term is a deliberately informal borrowing, not the formal computer-science definition of an oracle machine.

The retrocausal flavor of that picture — a later conscious outcome reaching back to shape which earlier pathway occurred — is the "abundance of future observation" idea already introduced, and caveated, on Entropic Expansion Cosmology's page. It's carried over here to explain how a single brain's pathways might get selected, not reintroduced as a new claim.

This is the furthest reach of anything on this site, and we want to be straightforward about it rather than either dismissing it or dressing it up: it's a claim of contact with named, real, deceased people, sourced to third-party recordings Darmos quotes rather than anything independently tested. As with every parapsychology claim on this site, it isn't accepted by mainstream science and is contested even within parapsychology's own literature. It's included because it's part of how Darmos's framework extends, not because it has been established.

Electronic voice phenomenon

Darmos treats electronic-voice-phenomenon ("spirit box") devices as a real-world channel for messages from people who have died. He reports on recording sessions run by two paranormal-investigation YouTubers, Steve Huff and Eman Rodriguez, and quotes a brief 2020 comment from a third investigator, Phillip Page, about his own equipment.

Darmos describes Huff's sessions as beginning shortly after the death of the actor and comedian Robin Williams, and separately as including sessions the investigators associated with the musician Michael Jackson; Rodriguez's sessions used a voice generator set to approximate Robin Williams' voice. Across both sets, he reports the audio as short phrases the investigators described as responsive and emotionally resonant — a description of what Darmos claims was recorded, not something this page can evaluate.

None of this is independently verified, and this page won't try to adjudicate it. What can be said plainly, out of respect for anyone who comes to this material with real loss of their own: EVP recordings rely on stretches of noise or fragmented audio that listeners interpret as speech, a pattern well documented outside parapsychology as ordinary auditory pattern-matching, and no recording of this kind has been accepted by mainstream science as evidence of communication with the deceased. To his credit, Darmos concedes in the same chapter that with spirit-box recordings it is impossible to know whether a pattern came from the hereafter or was induced by the observer.

From messaging to an engineering proposal

Twin oracles, and a path to superluminal communication

Everything below carries the same caveat weight as the psychokinesis claims earlier on this page, plus one more: it proposes a creative way around the no-communication theorem, a well-established result in quantum information theory holding that entanglement alone can never transmit information faster than light. To Darmos's credit, he doesn't dispute the theorem's mathematics — he proposes that trained psychokinesis supplies a non-random bias the theorem's proof assumes doesn't exist. That's a considerably larger claim than anything else on this page, and it rests entirely on every earlier psychokinesis claim being real and controllable.

Darmos's proposal is a pair of entangled "twin quantum oracles" — most concretely, entangled electron spins — measured non-invasively through the Faraday effect rather than a "sharp" Stern–Gerlach-style measurement, so the measurement doesn't destroy the entanglement it depends on. A skilled psychokinetic practitioner would bias the outcome on one side; if that bias is real, it should appear as a detectable pattern on the entangled partner's side too, however far away. As stated, it is at least a concrete, falsifiable design: either the pattern shows up or it doesn't.

Darmos distinguishes this from ordinary telepathy claims, and specifically from Dean Radin's suggestion that entanglement might underlie brain-to-brain telepathy, which he calls underspecified: human thought is carried by neurons rather than single particles, and any brain-to-brain entanglement wouldn't survive the body's ordinary turnover of its atoms.

A parallel idea, "superluminal video transmission": a camera whose pixels only display when they match an entangled partner pixel, with an observer's alleged micro-psychokinetic bias toward emotionally resonant images proposed to make a meaningful picture more likely to emerge than plain noise.

The interstellar-colonization framing below is the single most speculative sentence in this section — infrastructure for a civilization that doesn't yet have a working psychokinesis-based channel at any range, let alone across light-years. It shows how far Darmos will follow an idea once he trusts its logic, whatever one makes of where it lands.

Both ideas are framed as future infrastructure for interstellar colonization — a way to communicate with colonies light-years away without waiting years for an ordinary signal's round trip.
