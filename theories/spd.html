Elementary Spaces/Theories/Space Particle Dualism

Theory 2 of 6 · conceived 2005, on a car ride near Skala, Greece

Space is made of overlapping spheres

The book's second founding idea gives every particle its own small, complex-valued spherical surface instead of treating it as a point. Overlap enough of these "elementary spaces" and you get the three-dimensional space we live in — and, the book argues, the Standard Model's three forces fall out of the same structure without ever needing a Higgs field, an electroweak unification, or a grand-unified symmetry break. This page covers how that picture is built, what it predicts about the size of a particle, and the experiment proposed to actually measure one.

Where the theory starts

A particle is a surface, not a point

General relativity is built on point particles sitting inside a smooth, continuous spacetime. Space Particle Dualism starts from the opposite assumption: every particle carries its own "elementary space" — a small, two-dimensional spherical surface, sized directly by the particle's own mass-energy — — where is a new constant, scaled to the strength of electromagnetism rather than gravity. Billions of these surfaces, mostly belonging to the virtual particles constantly flickering in and out of the vacuum, overlap into the smooth three-dimensional space we move through. On this picture, space and matter were never two different things to begin with.

A later revision changes how to read that radius formula. It echoes the horizon radius of a black hole, but the Chromogravity chapter says elementary spaces should not be pictured as scaled-up black holes: black-hole gravity there is sourced by quark count, while an elementary space's size is set by energy.

Complex mass, and four kinds of matter

The book treats a particle's mass-energy as a complex number, , oscillating continuously between its real part a and its imaginary part b. A "measurement" just catches that oscillation at one instant. Sorting particles by the sign of each part gives four categories, not two: ordinary matter (both parts positive), antimatter (real positive, imaginary negative — matching John Wheeler's own observation that positrons behave like electrons running backward in time), and two categories with no Standard Model counterpart at all: exotic matter (both parts negative) and exotic antimatter. The book takes this literally — exotic matter isn't a metaphor for dark matter, it's a distinct particle type this framework predicts should exist.

An early wrong turn: radius versus surface area

The first version of this idea plugged the complex energy straight into the radius formula and squared it to get a surface area. Because squaring a complex number scrambles its signs — — this produced elementary spaces whose sign didn't match their own source particle's sign, which the book flags as an unacceptable extra degree of freedom with no physical meaning: a particle and its own surface are supposed to be the same fact, not two independently-signed ones.

The fix was to recognize that a complex sphere's radius has to be a real, absolute-value quantity, not a complex one — so the real and imaginary parts of the energy each get their own real radius, and those two radii add together to make the particle's full elementary space. Once that correction is made, the four sign-combinations above fall out cleanly, with no leftover ambiguity.

The surface is the particle

An earlier draft of the theory kept the particle and its elementary space as two separate things — a point riding on, or orbiting inside, its own surface. The book now treats that distinction as a confusion: there's no particle apart from the surface. What's usually called "rest mass" is just the rest-surface; what's usually called relativistic mass or energy is the same surface grown larger. The continuous appearing and disappearing of that surface — oscillating through every complex value that shares the same absolute size — is what generates the particle's wavefunction in the first place, not a hidden classical object bouncing around underneath it.

Where exotic matter is supposed to be

Exotic matter has negative inertial mass, which means — unlike ordinary matter — it repels its own kind. The book's proposal is that this repulsion locks exotic matter into a rigid lattice, a "Wigner crystal," spanning the entire universe: the very first structure to exist, on this account. Ordinary matter is then drawn toward the lattice points, flipping their net mass positive and triggering the collapse of the crystal into what becomes a sea of primordial black holes. This is a much more specific and unusual cosmological claim than most of what's on this page — mainstream cosmology has no comparable "exotic matter crystal" concept — and it's included here as the book's own account of where its own predicted particle type would actually be.

Where the Standard Model's structure comes from

Three forces, one nested geometry

The Standard Model's symmetry group, , is usually explained as the leftover shape of a larger, unbroken symmetry that existed at very high energy and then broke as the universe cooled. The book argues that no such breaking is needed — that the structure is already implied by which number systems each elementary space is built from.

Complex numbers have the symmetry group . Quaternions — a four-part extension of complex numbers, where multiplication order starts to matter — have the symmetry group , and contain the complex numbers inside them. Octonions — an eight-part extension where even grouping order stops mattering — have the symmetry group , which contains . The book's proposal is that photons and electrons are built from complex-valued elementary spaces, W- and Z-bosons and neutrinos from quaternionic ones, and quarks and gluons from octonionic ones — and because each number system already contains the simpler one, the nesting comes for free: quarks feel all three forces, W- and Z-bosons feel two, and electrons and photons feel only one, exactly matching what's observed, with no undiscovered particle zoo required to make it work.

The original, cruder version of this idea

The first attempt (2005–2006) simply assigned one-dimensional elementary spaces to , two-dimensional ones to , and the emergent overlap-space itself to . The book now lists its own flaws with that version: an elementary space doesn't gain new degrees of freedom just from which way it happens to point in 3D space, since physics is the same in every direction; and the emergent overlap-space isn't compactified the way true elementary spaces are, so its symmetry group should really have been the larger , not . Moving to quaternions and octonions for the weak and strong force was the fix.

This section's two specific rejections — of electroweak unification and of grand unification — deserve a stronger caveat than most of this site's claims. Grand unification (GUT) is genuinely speculative and untested; no experiment has ever probed its proposed energy scale. But the electroweak theory itself, which the book also calls "false," is one of the most thoroughly tested parts of the Standard Model — the W and Z bosons' masses, and the 2012 discovery of the Higgs boson, match its predictions precisely. The book's specific objection is that nobody has observed the symmetry being fully restored at high energy, which is a narrower and more exotic claim about the theory's deep ultraviolet behavior than most physicists would use the word "false" for.

Specifically: the book notes that electroweak theory predicts the W- and Z-bosons should become massless once collisions reach the symmetry-restoration energy of 246 GeV — but particle accelerators already reach 6,500 GeV, more than 25 times that, without anyone observing it. Likewise for grand unification: popular diagrams showing the three forces' coupling constants converging at high energy are, per the book, an oversimplification — the lines come close but don't actually meet, any more than two non-parallel lines crossing implies they were ever the same line.

How the book positions its own idea

Explaining mass and unification, by the book's own reckoning

Standard Model + Higgs mechanism Widely accepted · 1964 onward

The mainstream account: particles gain mass by interacting with the Higgs field. Accepted, but the book notes it predicts no particle masses on its own, and the symmetry group has to be separately replicated for each of the three generations of matter.

Electroweak unification Glashow, Salam, Weinberg · 1960s–70s

Rejected, in the narrow sense above: no observed symmetry restoration at 246 GeV, even at collision energies 25× higher. The underlying electroweak theory itself remains extremely well-tested; see the caveat above this table.

Grand Unified Theories (SU(5), SO(10)...) Georgi & Glashow · 1974 onward

Rejected: the three coupling constants approach each other at high energy but, per the book's reading, don't actually converge — closer to non-parallel lines crossing than forces merging. Never tested directly either way.

String theory Compactified extra dimensions

Rejected as an unnecessary complication: needs 26 compactified dimensions once supersymmetry — which would have simplified that number — turned out not to match any observed particle. The book argues spheres resolve point-particle infinities as well as strings do, without the extra machinery.

Twistor theory Roger Penrose

A direct inspiration for elementary spaces, but the book abandoned literally identifying its own surfaces with Penrose's twistors or Riemann spheres — see the next section for why.

Octonionic Standard Model Cohl Furey · 2014 onward

Genuinely similar tools — complex numbers, quaternions, octonions — but a more ambitious aim: one 64-dimensional tensor product meant to unify gravity, quantum mechanics and the Standard Model at once, rather than SPD's separate, independent use of each number system.

Space Particle Dualism Sky Darmos (2005) · this page

The book's own proposal: the Standard Model's nested symmetry falls directly out of complex, quaternionic and octonionic elementary spaces, with no symmetry breaking and no predicted-but-unseen particles required.

A short but important correction

A necessary caution: math tools aren't physical objects

A complex number can always be pictured sitting on a Riemann sphere, and it's tempting to picture an elementary space the same way — as a literal sphere with a point orbiting on its surface, representing the particle. The book tried exactly that in its earliest form, deliberately building on Penrose's twistor theory. It doesn't work: if the orbiting point is the particle, then its orbit speed has to increase as the elementary space grows, and once the elementary space's radius exceeds the particle's own wavelength, that orbit speed would have to exceed the speed of light.

The fix is to stop treating the Riemann sphere as a physical object at all. At any given instant, an elementary space's entire surface has exactly one complex value for its area — not four differently signed quadrants with something physically sweeping between them. The Riemann sphere is only a way of representing that single number geometrically, the same way a spacetime diagram represents an impulse without the diagram itself being a real four-dimensional object anyone lives inside. The book is explicit that both general relativity and twistor theory can fall into this same trap: it's easy to mistake a useful mathematical picture for the physical thing it was drawn to describe.

The same reasoning rules out a separate suggestion the book records receiving — that vibrating strings could be layered onto elementary spaces, the way string theory vibrates strings in compactified dimensions. Since a particle's whole energy comes from its surface continuously appearing and disappearing, not from any internal vibration, adding a string to that surface wouldn't change anything about what's actually being measured.

From geometry to the hierarchy problem

The density of space, and why gravity is so weak

Most approaches to quantum gravity work with entities at the Planck scale, which makes them nearly impossible to test. Before 2016, elementary spaces in this framework were thought to be smaller than the Planck length too — which turns out to be self-defeating, because vacuum fluctuations shouldn't appear closer together than one Planck length apart, so anything smaller than that can't overlap with its neighbors densely enough to build a gapless patchwork of space.

How big is an elementary space, really?

The fix was to stop using the ordinary gravitational constant to size elementary spaces, and use a new constant instead — scaled up to the strength of electromagnetism rather than gravity: . Equivalently, — the ratio between electromagnetism's coupling constant and gravity's, which comes out to about 1.24×1036. Swapping for grows an electron's elementary space from an absurdly small 1.35×10-57 m — using ordinary gravity — up to about 10-21 m, just three orders of magnitude below the smallest distances particle physics has ever directly probed.

Vacuum energy, without renormalization

Vacuum fluctuations can, in principle, have any energy at all, and the more energetic they are, the more briefly they exist. Standard quantum field theory only gets a finite value for the vacuum's energy density by applying renormalization — without it, the sum diverges to infinity. The book takes a different route: it quantizes those fluctuations directly, first approximately, by allowed lifetimes, then more precisely, by allowed wavelengths in multiples of the Planck length, and sums the (deliberately truncated) series using the Euler–Mascheroni constant as a correction term. Counting only virtual photons, that lands on roughly 6.1×10116 J/m3 — and higher still once the other sixteen Standard Model particle types, and an assumed share for dark matter, are added in.

Granular dimensionality, and the hierarchy problem

How three-dimensional a patch of space feels depends on how many other elementary spaces each one overlaps with — call that number n: . Plugging the vacuum energy figures above into n gives a value in the range of 1066–1068 connections per elementary space, which pushes D to within roughly 10-34 of exactly 3. That's the entire explanation for why gravity is so weak on this account: gravity isn't a separately weak force being diluted by something else, it is that residual gap between D and a perfectly smooth 3. The book's own numbers land gravity at around 1035 times weaker than electromagnetism — close to, but not quite, the 1036 ratio the theory's other constants would predict, a discrepancy the book flags rather than rounds away.

One consequence the book draws from treating gravity this way: because only differences in density cause gravitational pull, a substance spread perfectly evenly through space — like the vacuum energy itself — can't have any gravitational effect at all, no matter how large that energy is. That sidesteps the vacuum catastrophe that a genuinely global, force-carrying "graviton" would otherwise run into.

From formula to experiment

Testing it: probing a particle's own size

An elementary space's diameter grows with its particle's energy. Set that diameter equal to the particle's own wavelength, and you get a specific, calculable "critical frequency" — the point where a particle's own extended size should start to matter for how it behaves, rather than being negligible. For photons, that works out to: , a frequency of about 3.3×1024 Hz and a wavelength of about 9.0×10-17 m — for comparison, visible light sits around 1014–1015 Hz, and typical gamma rays around 1019 Hz.

Repeating the calculation for an electron's de Broglie wavelength gives a critical wavelength of 9.0065857×10-17 m — agreeing with the photon's own critical wavelength to six digits, a strikingly tight match given that the two calculations don't even share all the same constants. The book proves this isn't a coincidence that a heavier particle could beat: solving the general case shows the two critical wavelengths only converge exactly in the limit where a particle's mass goes to zero and its speed goes to — meaning no particle with any rest mass can ever out-resolve a photon at this game, however close it gets.

Why a heavier particle doesn't automatically win

Counter-intuitively, the Higgs boson — far heavier than an electron — has a longer critical wavelength (about 8.18×10-16 m) than the electron does. That's because a heavier particle reaches its own critical point at a much lower speed; it doesn't need to approach light speed the way a light particle does. Resolving power tracks how close a particle gets to , not how heavy it is — which is exactly why only a massless particle, or the lightest possible mass approaching light speed, can ever reach the photon's own limit.

Direct single-particle measurement tops out around 9×10-17 m — LIGO's laser interferometers already measure distance differences an order of magnitude smaller than that, around 10-18 m, but by comparing waves against each other rather than firing a single particle through a gap.

The proposed slit experiment

The most direct test would fire photons above the critical frequency through a gap the size of the critical wavelength itself, and see whether they get stuck — something ordinary quantum mechanics says shouldn't happen for any gap wider than about 10-22 m. That precise a gap is far beyond current fabrication, but a monoatomic lattice of solid hydrogen — kept below its 14.01 K melting point, which is easy to achieve — offers a naturally occurring, atom-spaced gap of 2.2858×10-10 m. Solving for that gap width gives a required photon frequency of about 8.45×1030 Hz.

The proposed setup: a spectrometer, a solid-hydrogen plate, and a photon detector, flown in outer space (Earth-based gamma-ray detectors only infer wavelength indirectly, from the atmospheric Cherenkov radiation a gamma ray produces, which isn't precise enough here). If high-frequency photons that ordinary quantum mechanics predicts should pass straight through instead get stuck at this lattice spacing, that would be direct evidence for the elementary space radius this theory predicts.

Status: proposed, not yet run. The highest-frequency gamma rays ever detected sit around 1030 Hz, by the book's own account — roughly an order of magnitude below what this experiment needs, and the book itself notes that its limited recognition so far makes it unlikely any space agency will attempt it soon.

Where rest mass — and the three generations — come from

Mass out of light

If elementary spaces explain particles, where does ordinary rest mass — the electron's, say — come from in the first place? The book has revised its answer to this twice.

Two abandoned versions

The original 2005 idea borrowed the black-hole analogy directly: bundles of massless photons could get trapped the way light gets trapped in an event horizon, forming a rest-mass particle out of pure light. The book now rejects this on its own terms — in this framework photons carry no quarks and so exert no gravity, meaning they can't even form the "quasi-black holes" this theory otherwise allows for, let alone a genuine event horizon.

A 2015 revision modeled the massless/massive difference as one of dimensionality — one-dimensional elementary spaces for massless particles, two-dimensional ones for massive particles — inferred from the extra spin states a massive particle carries. That was dropped once the nested-symmetry picture above required both the complex (U(1)) and quaternionic (SU(2)) types of elementary space to produce massless and massive particles each.

The current version: merging

The book's present answer is that a massive particle forms when two massless elementary spaces merge — which both halts them, the way an event horizon would, and adds a dimension, giving the result its extra spin degrees of freedom, all without invoking a Higgs field. Because it's the specific structure of an elementary space, not the presence of mass itself, that generates charge, the resulting particle picks up charge in the process too — unlike a hypothetical light-only black hole, which never could.

Why three generations?

Muons and tauons are identical to electrons in every way except mass, appear only briefly in high-energy collisions, and decay almost immediately — a puzzle particle physics has carried since their discovery. The book's proposal: they aren't separately fundamental at all. A muon is modeled as two electron-elementary-spaces merged together, with one positron emitted to keep the net charge at exactly −1; a tauon is three merged electrons, with two positrons emitted. Because electrons are fermions and resist sitting close together, forcing several of them into contact takes a large amount of energy — which the book offers as the qualitative reason a muon ends up 207 times heavier than a single electron, despite being "made of" the same stuff.

The book takes this a good deal further in its next chapter, Charge Mass Dualism, turning this qualitative merging story into a quantitative prediction of the muon and tauon masses — the homepage's own summary of that chapter reports a match to better than 99.9% against the measured values, including the puzzle already flagged on the homepage's Problems section, the Koide formula. If the three generations depended instead on some arbitrarily-shaped compactified dimension, as string theory would have it, the book argues there'd be no reason to expect any such pattern in their masses at all.
