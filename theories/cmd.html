Mass is a side effect of charge

Space Particle Dualism explained gravity as a side effect of charge. This chapter asks whether inertia is too. An electron's calculated self-energy matches its measured mass to seven significant figures, and pushing the same idea further predicts the muon and tauon as merged electron "elementary spaces," eventually clearing 99.9% agreement after a chain of corrections. Few attempts to explain the particle generations get this close to the measured numbers. This page walks through that chain, a related reinterpretation of how neutrinos shape-shift, and a granularity prediction Darmos himself concedes can never be measured.

Where the theory starts

Mass as a leftover of a particle's own charge

Chromogravity, covered in a later chapter, argues that gravity is a side effect of charge and hypercharge rather than a fundamental force. This chapter asks whether the same logic covers inertia. There's a rough pattern: mesons and baryons, tied to the strong force, are heavier than electrons, tied to electromagnetism; neutrinos, tied to the weak force, have almost no mass; and every massless particle in the Standard Model is also chargeless. Could a particle's mass just measure how strongly it couples to a force, rather than being an independent property?

The specific mechanism Darmos reaches for is self-energy — the energy stored in a charged particle's own field, from its interaction with the surrounding quantum vacuum. The idea is old: J. J. Thomson proposed it in 1881, before the quantum vacuum was a concept, calling the medium the field interacted with the "aether."

An electron's mass, entirely from its own field

The calculation was worked out by the German physicist Klaus Lux and shared with the author by personal correspondence in 2018. Lux modeled the electron as a small charged sphere with a field reaching to infinity — mathematically a spherical capacitor whose outer plate sits infinitely far away — and derived the energy stored in that field. Plugging in the numbers gives 9.109382913×10-31 kg against a measured electron mass of 9.109383561×10-31 kg — agreement to seven significant figures.

Darmos himself raises one caveat: the classical electron radius is defined using the electron's mass, so this isn't a derivation of the mass out of nothing. What it shows is that the electron's mass and its field self-energy can safely be treated as the same quantity — the premise the rest of this chapter builds on.

The 4/3 problem, and who else worked on this

Between 1882 and 1904, several physicists pursued the same idea — most notably Hendrik Lorentz — and concluded that a moving electron's electromagnetic mass should be of its field energy divided by . Wilhelm Wien (1900) and Max Abraham (1902) then argued this meant all mass, everywhere, was electromagnetic in origin. The awkward factor of 4/3 violates special relativity, and Fermi, Dirac, Rohrlich, and Schwinger each showed how it disappears in a properly relativistic calculation. In 2011, Valery Morozov pinned the correction down precisely: a moving charged sphere carries a flux of non-electromagnetic energy whose impulse is exactly one-third of the sphere's own electromagnetic impulse — which Darmos suggests is the same effect behind the extra factor of one-half in Klaus Lux's own calculation above.

Darmos argues that "for unclear reasons," electromagnetic explanations of mass have been set aside in favor of the Higgs mechanism — which, unlike the self-energy picture, doesn't predict any particle's specific mass.

How well does force strength track mass?

If mass is a side effect of charge, the ratio between two particles' masses should roughly match the ratio between the strengths of the forces they carry. Lined up naively, the fit is suggestive but loose: the electron-neutrino is about 7.3 million times lighter than the electron, while the weak-to-electromagnetic coupling ratio is only about 26,600 — off by a factor of 160. The proton fares better, off by a factor of 4.5 from the electromagnetic-to-strong ratio.

Closing that gap requires the weak coupling constant itself, calculated more precisely from the ratio of two particles' decay times — the weakly-decaying boson against the strongly-decaying boson — which yields . Why the neutron outweighs the proton

Down-quarks carry slightly more mass than up-quarks, so the neutron — two downs and an up — is heavier than the proton — two ups and a down. The gap, only about 2.5 electron masses, is Darmos's clue: a free neutron decays into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino, and those escaping particles need enough shared energy to carry matching momentum, which works out to roughly that same surplus. Why is the neutron the heavier one? Darmos's answer is entropy: beta decay turns one particle into three, overwhelmingly the likelier direction; running it backward requires three specific particles to converge on one point at once, which happens only under the density of a neutron star or a black hole.

An unsolved piece: the W- and Z-bosons

Darmos is candid that this model alone has nothing to say about why the W- and Z-bosons are so heavy. He offers one tentative bridge back to Space Particle Dualism's critical photon energy: two merging critical-energy photons, divided by the strong coupling at the so-called Z-pole, land within 37% of the actual W/Z average mass (54% of the Higgs boson's mass); a single critical-energy photon alone does better, landing within 74% (93% of the Higgs mass). Both are order-of-magnitude matches at best, and Darmos treats the gap as possibly explainable by the running of coupling rather than a settled result — the one calculation in the chapter that leans on SPD rather than self-energy alone. By the same logic in reverse, gluons would have an almost immeasurably small mass, around 5×10-42 kg, too small to slow them down at all.

The comparison below uses the Higgs mechanism as a foil, so it's worth being precise about what's well-tested. The Higgs boson and its role in electroweak symmetry breaking are firmly established — its 2012 discovery at the LHC matched predictions closely. What Darmos disputes is narrower: whether the Higgs mechanism explains ordinary fermion masses, given that it supplies no predictions for them. That's a genuine, open gap in the Standard Model, not a rejection of a well-confirmed result.

Electromagnetic mass theory Thomson, Lorentz, Wien, Abraham · 1881–1904

The historical predecessor this chapter revives: mass as field self-energy. It predicted no particle masses beyond the electron, and was set aside once the flawed 4/3 factor (later resolved) made it look inconsistent with relativity.

Higgs mechanism Standard Model · 1964 onward

Well-confirmed as an account of electroweak symmetry breaking, but — as Darmos notes and is widely acknowledged — it gives no first-principles prediction for any fermion's mass; those are free parameters put in by hand.

Koide's formula Yoshio Koide · 1981

An empirical relationship between the electron, muon, and tauon masses with no accepted derivation in the Standard Model — the puzzle the next section tries to explain.

From speculation to a number

Predicting the muon and the tauon

Space Particle Dualism's own account of rest mass speculated that the muon and tauon are two or three electron elementary spaces merged into one, with the surplus charge escaping as one or two positrons. This chapter turns that qualitative story into a predicted number, measured against the Koide formula — a relationship the Japanese physicist Yoshio Koide noticed in 1981 between the three charged leptons' masses, with no accepted explanation in the Standard Model: .

Treating the muon and tauon as literally 2 and 3 merged electron charges, and plugging their masses straight into the electromagnetic mass formula above, gives only a 58% match to that ratio. Darmos then works through several refinements in sequence, each correcting the last:

The chain of corrections, step by step

Accounting for the polarized-vacuum cloud. A stronger charge polarizes a larger cloud of virtual positrons around it, and that cloud's volume — not just the source charge — contributes mass. Scaling mass by the cube of the charge count instead of the square brings the Koide match to 92%.

Accounting for confinement. Two electrons can share space if their spins are opposite, but a third crowds the other two, shrinking the effective confinement radius. Correcting for this pushes the Koide match to 98%, and the raw predicted masses to about 201 for the muon (97% of the true 207 ) and 3,435 for the tauon (99% of the true 3,477 ) — after multiplying both by , a factor Darmos points out because it fits almost too well.

Running of coupling. Using the electromagnetic coupling constant evaluated at the classical electron radius, rather than its low-energy value, sharpens the muon to 205.44 (99.36%) and the tauon to 3,510.13 (99.06%).

Binding energy. Merging charges releases negative potential energy, which reduces the resting mass slightly — but that energy has to be supplied again as input when a muon or tauon is created in a collision, and experiments typically measure the input energy. Accounting for both versions moves the numbers around (down to 98.15%/99.30% for the true rest mass, back up to 99.43%/98.82% for the "apparent," input-energy version) without changing the picture much.

Emitted positrons. Charge conservation requires one or two positrons to escape during the merger, carrying away some of that input energy. Subtracting their mass brings the final figures to 206.95 for the muon (99.91%) and 3,515.59 for the tauon (98.91%) — a combined Koide-ratio agreement of 99.85%.

Each step above is physically motivated on its own terms, but the sequence as a whole is a story of free parameters added one at a time, each chosen partly because it moved the result closer to the masses already known from measurement. Darmos's own calculation of the odds of reaching this precision by chance — 0.000038% — says how unlikely the final digits are to line up this well if the model were arbitrary; it is not an independent, blind test of the model. Treat the closeness of fit with that in mind.

Darmos also notes a possible independent hint: the CERN and Brookhaven muon g−2 experiments (1959–2006), confirmed by Fermilab in 2021, measured the muon's magnetic moment slightly off the Standard Model's predicted value — a discrepancy whose size, and whether it is new physics at all, is still debated because the Standard Model prediction itself is hard to calculate. Darmos speculates that some of the gap could come from the same potential energy this chapter uses to build the muon, though he doesn't work the connection through.

Back to the Similar-Worlds Interpretation

Flavor mixing, real and apparent

An electron can be in superposition of spinning up and down, or of going left and right — but never of being an electron and being a positron. Some neutral particles break that rule: neutrinos are permanently in superposition of electron-, muon-, and tau-flavor, and some neutral mesons (the K-mesons, or kaons) exist only as a superposition of two quark compositions. Standard quantum mechanics doesn't explain why some properties can mix like this and others can't.

The Similar-Worlds Interpretation offers a candidate answer, and shows the framework's pieces working together: we co-exist in every world that's currently indistinguishable from every other, including worlds where a particle's very identity differs, as long as nothing yet reveals which is "real." How long such an identity-superposition can last is set by the energy-time uncertainty relation, , where is the energy difference the environment would need to detect. For a hypothetical proton-neutron superposition, that works out to about 2.2×10-24 seconds — far too brief for the proton's easily detectable charge to stay hidden, which is why charge conservation holds so reliably in practice. Neutrinos have no charge to give them away, and interact so weakly that a lead block a light-year thick would stop only half of them; a neutrino's flavor-identity can stay superposed for roughly 1015 seconds or more, which is what makes flavor oscillation — a neutrino "shape-shifting" as it travels — possible at all.

Mass without oscillation

Even when a particle's identity-superposition is too short-lived to oscillate — an electron can't become a muon — Darmos argues a sliver of the other identity still leaks into its effective mass, in proportion to how much of a full identity-cycle elapses in that brief window. Applying these factors to the previous section's tauon calculation initially makes things worse (down to 98.53% agreement), until one more correction is added: extrapolating the muon's own small remaining error, scaled by the tauon-to-muon mass ratio, up to the tauon. That step is explicitly a plausibility check rather than a derivation, but it lands the tauon mass at 3,476.83 — a 99.99% agreement with the CODATA-recommended tauon mass, and a match to four of the five individual published tau-mass measurements (1992–2020) within their own margins of error. Only the CODATA average itself misses, which Darmos attributes to its stated uncertainty being a purely statistical figure across five very different experiments, understating the real systematic spread.

As a loose hint rather than a proof, Darmos points out that the same factor from the electron's own mixing shows up again in the ratio between the observed and predicted values in the muon g−2 experiments discussed above (1.153 observed against 1.159 predicted) — suggestive, but one numerical coincidence, not a second derivation.

A real effect, applied to a hypothetical particle

The hidden energy of spin

Earlier, the leftover gap in the muon and tauon mass predictions was tentatively attributed to "probably spin." This section calculates that contribution. The underlying physics is the Zeeman effect — the well-established energy splitting between aligned and anti-aligned spin states, familiar from atomic spectroscopy — applied to the merging electron charges that form a muon or tauon.

The electric field on each merging charge, and the speed it must move at (drawn from the binding energy calculated earlier, split evenly between the charges), give a magnetic field, and from there a spin-alignment energy via , where is the electron's own magnetic moment. For the muon, this closes a 97.19% match against the residual gap the earlier chapter had left unexplained. For the tauon — after folding in the extra scaling factors for the larger polarized-vacuum cloud, the tauon's own confinement correction, and the flavor-mixing factor from the previous section — the match is 89.77%.

Net effect: folding spin energy into the muon and tauon masses raises the average accuracy of both predictions from 99.41% (using only the corrections from the two previous sections) to 99.914% — 206.7734 for the muon against a measured 206.7683 (99.998%), and 3,483.04 for the tauon against a measured 3,477.22 ± 0.23 (99.83%).

Walking back an assumption, then reaching further

One rest surface, and a speculative leap into relativity

Earlier chapters scaled each particle type's elementary-space size by a different constant, tied to the strength of the force it carries. This chapter reverses that choice, simplifying the picture: if the three forces converge in strength at high energy, as grand unification proposes, then every particle's underlying, "naked" surface ought to be exactly the same size — and the different masses we observe come from how strongly each type of charge polarizes the vacuum around it (the ordinary running of coupling), not from differently-sized fundamental surfaces. That gives a single "rest surface" formula for fermions, , and a matching circumference formula for a massless boson like the photon — bosons, with one complex dimension to their elementary space rather than a fermion's two, get no full real surface at all.

A more speculative reach: deriving relativity itself

Darmos then tries something more ambitious. A moving charge's magnetic field is, classically, its electric field seen through Lorentz contraction — the textbook explanation of how relativity makes magnets work. If mass is electromagnetic self-energy, Darmos argues, the relativistic increase in a particle's mass at high speed is that same magnetic-energy increase. Since an elementary space's size tracks its particle's energy, a fast-moving particle's elementary space should grow too — uniformly, in every direction — which raises a puzzle: length contraction happens only along the direction of motion. Darmos's resolution is that the growth is only apparent from outside the moving frame; from inside, every relevant constant grows together and cancels out. Reinterpreting that outside view as an effective slowing of light inside the moving frame, he suggests, could generate length contraction and time dilation together, as a package.

This is the most speculative material on the page, and Darmos is unusually candid about it: he says this chain of reasoning "would have to be shown in a more rigorous way" before it could count as an actual derivation of special relativity, and at one point acknowledges his own argument as possibly circular — "this may sound like a tautology, and it might be one." It's included as a research direction Darmos points toward, not a completed result.

From formula to (an admittedly tiny) experiment

Testing it: a prediction too small to measure

Granular space should make any particle's path slightly longer than a straight line, through microscopic zig-zagging between overlapping elementary spaces: . For a one-meter path, that extra length comes out to roughly 8×10-37 m — a particle would need to travel about 1.24×1036 meters before the zig-zagging added up to one extra meter. For light crossing the visible universe, using Darmos's own roughly-42-trillion-year age estimate, the total extra path is on the order of 10-15 m.

Status: real prediction, not a measurable one. Unlike the experiments proposed elsewhere on this site — unbuilt, but buildable in principle — Darmos's conclusion here is blunter: "its incredible smallness makes it untestable." If the effect were larger, it might show up as an anomalous redshift in the most distant objects, but at this scale no proposed instrument could isolate it from ordinary noise. It's to his credit that he says so plainly.

A consistency check on the hierarchy problem

The chapter closes by revisiting Space Particle Dualism's hierarchy-problem estimate with a far more careful count. Rather than treating every virtual particle as an identical photon, this version weights each type by its own elementary-space size, sums across the Standard Model's 17 fundamental particle types and their exotic-matter counterparts, and counts the individual quarks inside each of hundreds of known mesons and baryons. The refined estimate lands between 1.4×1069 and 3.4×1069 connections per elementary space, depending on how compound particles are counted — close to, but still under, the roughly 1070 ceiling the theory requires for space to feel convincingly three-dimensional. Darmos is explicit about why it matters: "it is good that our result here did not exceed this value. If it did, the theory would be in serious trouble." The same calculation shows that massive fermions dominate this connectivity almost entirely — massless photons alone contribute a negligible 3.4×1060. Darmos's formulae chapter, after this recount, lists the gap between the granular dimensionality and exactly 3 as 10-35, against the roughly 10-34 on the Space Particle Dualism page; so far as this guide could find, Darmos doesn't say the refined count closes the 1035-versus-1036 discrepancy noted there.
