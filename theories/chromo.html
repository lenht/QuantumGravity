Theory 6 of 6 · conceived 19 May 2017, extended through 25 May 2026

Gravity depends on what you're weighing

Space Particle Dualism made gravity a side effect of charge; Charge Mass Dualism made mass one too. If gravity counts quarks, not kilograms, then a kilogram of iron and a kilogram of water shouldn't pull quite alike, and Newton's constant isn't constant. This is the most testable part of the framework: Darmos builds a rewritten law of gravity on the idea and checks it against two centuries of big-G measurements, drop-tower and pendulum experiments, Mercury's orbit, neutron stars and binary pulsars. This page separates the published data Darmos re-analyses, experiments others ran, and experiments he ran himself, and ends with what Darmos proposes but nobody has built.

Where the theory starts

Inertia and gravity come apart

The equivalence principle — that inertial and gravitational mass are equal, so everything falls alike — is among the most precisely tested principles in physics. The best current test, the MICROSCOPE satellite, compared titanium and platinum alloys in orbit and reported no violation in its 2022 final results, at a few parts in 1015. Darmos's claimed material differences are of order 10-3–10-2; this chapter does not discuss MICROSCOPE. It argues instead that balance-type experiments (including the classic Eötvös ones) can't reveal the effect, as described below, and looks for it in other data.

In Space Particle Dualism gravity is a side effect of charge (strong hypercharge, chiefly), and in Charge Mass Dualism mass is too. One consequence is immediate: a photon carries no charge, so it has no gravitational mass, contrary to general relativity. Darmos therefore insists on a distinction. Inertial mass is what a balance measures, and it changes with energy; gravitational mass is what sources a gravitational field. The two are usually treated as one number, which Darmos calls an assumption: before Newton, nobody knew mass had anything to do with gravity.

Electric charge doesn't grow when a particle speeds up, so on this account gravity shouldn't grow when an electron is accelerated either. Which parts of an atom's inertial mass, then, are purely energetic and not tied to any gravitating charge? Electron kinetic energy and electron masses are negligible next to the nucleons'. The large one is binding energy — the “mass defect” that makes a nucleus weigh less than its separate protons and neutrons.

Iron has the highest mass defect per nucleon of any element. Its 26 protons and 30 neutrons, taken separately, would weigh 56.44938 atomic mass units; an iron atom weighs 55.845. That is a defect of . If gravity tracks the number of nucleons (that is, quarks) rather than the weight on a balance, a kilogram of iron gravitates about 1% more than a kilogram of hydrogen, and gravity between hydrogen atoms comes out roughly 1% weaker than Newton's formula predicts from their mass. Darmos says there is strong evidence for this; the rest of the page is that evidence.

Why big-G would look inconstant

Cavendish's 1798 experiment gave a value equivalent to 6.74×10-11 in modern units, and every measurement since has differed from it and from the others — even as precision improved, scatter between laboratories stayed far larger than their stated error bars. This chapter sets out to explain that scatter. Darmos's reading is that measured G is a geometric mean of source-mass and test-mass values, each material having its own (see the Cavendish section). Earlier explanations, such as G varying over time, failed: a 2015 analysis by Schlamminger, Gundlach and Newman found no periodicity, and three torsion balances of the same material at one site gave the same value across ten years.

Why hasn't anyone noticed a material dependence? Darmos's answer: the differences are small (about 0.1–1%); most experiments vary only the test masses; and the Eötvös experiment — the one Einstein pointed to as evidence for the equivalence principle — compares two masses on a single balance, which Darmos says cannot show the effect (see below). He also notes that Heyl (1926–30) and Pontikis (1971) each saw composition differences and didn't pursue them.

Where Fischbach's "fifth force" fits

In 1986, Ephraim Fischbach and colleagues found that Eötvös's own results correlated with baryon number per unit mass. They framed this as a medium-range fifth force added on top of the equivalence principle, and had to multiply the expected effect by an arbitrary coupling to match. Darmos agrees the correlation is real, but argues the effect expected under chromogravity is about a million times larger than what Eötvös measured, and that the residual is explained by how a torsion balance is built — see the Eötvös section.

Two forces, two ranges

Two gravities — and why neutrinos don't have one

This rewrite of what gravity counts stands or falls with the evidence in the sections that follow, not with its elegance.

G-values below are in units of 10-11 m3 kg-1 s-2 unless noted. The most precise laboratory measurements of G — Gundlach's in 2000 and two from the Huazhong University group in 2018 — all used stainless-steel masses, giving 6.674215(92), 6.674184(78) and 6.674484(78). Their average is 6.67429(17), against the CODATA recommendation of 6.67430(15), and the mode and median of all steel measurements is 6.6742. Leaving out the second Huazhong value, which uses a different method and disagrees with Gundlach's, gives Darmos's “steel” value of 6.674200(22). Grade-304 stainless steel is 0.08% carbon, 2% manganese, 0.045% phosphorus, 0.03% sulphur, 1% silicon, 19% chromium and 9.5% nickel, which lets Darmos back out a value for iron itself, GFe = 6.674280(22). Newton's constant is, on this account, the mass-gravity constant of iron.

From iron Darmos works down to hydrogen by counting nucleons rather than weighing them: , with isotope-weighted iron (inertial mass 55.845144 u, gravitational 56.358288 u — a later passage in the chapter gives 56.358157 u). The refined results are GH = 6.613511, Gp = 6.617210 and Gn = 6.608201 — the neutron has the same quark count as a proton but more mass, so less gravity per kilogram, about 1% below iron. For any other element, , where γ is an in-frame Lorentz factor for internal or thermal motion. Adding electrons adds inertia without adding quarks, so it lowers G per kilogram; removing them raises it.

Electrogravity is gravity sourced by electric charge instead of quark count, and it is far weaker: , about 4.83×10-13 in the same units. Darmos argues only chromogravity is long-range. Virtual gluons carry colour charge and emit further gluons, so their “journey” continues past a single virtual particle's lifetime; virtual photons and W and Z bosons don't do that. Taking the lowest energy a virtual particle can have while its elementary space still reaches the Planck length, Emin = 3.957848975×10-28 J, gives . The ranges are therefore infinite for chromogravity, about 40 m for electrogravity, and about 10-15–10-16 m for the weak force.

One consequence is that neutrinos exert no gravity. They have inertial mass but no quarks, no electric charge, and a weak-force range far too short to matter (they are also too fast to clump). Darmos takes this as showing how different inertial and gravitational mass are. He then restates gravity as a cardinal force, one that counts charges regardless of sign or colour. Its constants, in m3 kg s-2: GB = 1.8513020×10-64 per baryon; the chromogravity constant per quark (a ninth of GB); and the electrogravity constant G|Q| = 1.3509603×10-66. Absolute charges are counted: a proton is 5/3, a neutron 4/3, an electron 1; a baryon carries three quarks and a meson two, so mesons gravitate too. Darmos predicts that once accepted, chromogravity will simply be called gravity and Newton's G the mass-gravity constant of iron. Electrogravity, it adds, matters mainly for exotic matter, since electron gases repel far too strongly for it to be measured.

Photons carry no quarks and so exert no gravity, though as the next section explains, they still feel it. Entropic Expansion Cosmology's page already builds its argument against Hawking radiation on exactly this point.

The true masses of the Earth, Moon and Sun

Inertial masses inferred from gravity change once composition is accounted for. Darmos uses atomic rather than mass abundances, the gravitational-to-inertial ratio for each element, and GFe as the baseline. His own results: Earth 5.9722 → 5.9748×1024 kg (+0.043%); Moon 7.347×1022 kg (+0.068%); Sun 1.98847×1030 → (2.00389 ± 0.00007)×1030 kg (+0.78%; a recomputation from the Sun's composition gives +0.76%). The Sun changes most because it is almost entirely hydrogen and helium. Darmos adds that a direct test, detonating a bomb on the Moon and watching its orbit, is unnecessary because laboratory tests exist.

Rewriting the Planck units

Replacing G with the chromogravity constant, Darmos builds a new Planck-like length that isn't arbitrary. A photon can't become a black hole in this framework, because its elementary space outgrows its wavelength, so the Planck length loses its usual “photon into micro-black-hole” reading. The shortest length one can measure directly with a single photon is the critical wavelength, 9×10-17 m, the figure on the Space Particle Dualism page. And elementary spaces should not be thought of as scaled-up black holes, because black holes here depend on quark count while elementary spaces depend on energy; the factor of 2 in the elementary-space radius formula is folded into a Planck-like unit, though Darmos ends by calling it only “not unreasonable” and saying experiments will decide.

That last point is a later revision. The Space Particle Dualism page and the homepage card still describe elementary spaces by analogy with a black hole's event horizon, which was Darmos's earlier framing.

What changes, and what doesn't

The laws, rewritten in quarks

Nothing here changes a prediction for everyday gravity except by the ~1% (and smaller) composition effects the rest of the page examines. Whether those effects exist is the question.

Newton's law becomes , with quark counts in place of masses. For baryons there is a practical mass form, using GH and a baryon-number-to-mass correction; mesons and tetraquarks use the quark form; isotopes use the isotope-weighted form. Electrogravity has the same shape, , with a 40 m cut-off.

Escape velocity then depends on composition, not on mass: . What matters is kX, the quarks-per-kilogram ratio of the falling object (an iron atom has 168 quarks, 56 nucleons times three). Removing electrons raises the escape velocity; removing nucleons lowers it. So there is no single Schwarzschild radius, only one per composition, and the same goes for gravitational acceleration. Darmos compares electromagnetism, where escape velocity depends on the charge-to-mass ratio: the rewritten equations look odd next to Newton's but ordinary next to Coulomb's.

Historically, Darmos argues that the equivalence principle rests on what it calls Galileo's educated guess. Viviani's biography places the Pisa experiments in 1589–92, and Darmos says an earlier version of Galileo's law — that bodies of the same material falling through the same medium fall at the same speed — is exactly this theory's law for objects made of quarks.

On relativity, Darmos claims that the Lorentz and Schwarzschild factors cancel, so the non-relativistic results above stay valid. His method treats the Schwarzschild factor as having the same structure as the Lorentz factor with the escape velocity as the speed, and expands it as a series rather than solving kinetic energy equals potential energy directly, which it says gives a wrong answer. Force, potential energy and acceleration grow without limit as the escape velocity approaches the speed of light. Light bending is a check the two theories nearly share: general relativity gives 1.751″ for light grazing the Sun, this framework 1.749″ using the Sun's quark count, against a best cited measurement (a 1973 eclipse) of 1.66 ± 0.1″. Darmos says plainly that this is too coarse to discriminate.

Two more things belong here because Darmos walks them back or tests them himself. Gravitational time dilation was briefly suspected in October 2024 to be composition-dependent; on 23 October 2024 Darmos concluded it is not, because dilation tracks vacuum density — the uniform growth of elementary spaces described in the earlier pages — rather than gravitational acceleration. Expressed in mass form it reads , where GM is the source body's constant and Gn the neutron's, the neutron being the particle that feels the least gravity. Darmos also proposed that G varies with altitude, since a stronger background field would raise granular dimensionality and weaken gravity, and then checked 14 measurement sites between 5 m and 2,258 m elevation. He found no correlation. His explanation is that a uniform rescaling of all elementary spaces in a region is invisible from inside and looks like time dilation from outside. It is a clear case of Darmos testing and dropping one of his own ideas, the kind of self-checking that gives the rest of the chapter more weight.

Photons and electrons feel gravity but don't source it

Quarkless test masses still fall. Darmos gives them a passive gravitational charge, arising from higher-order Feynman diagrams in which virtual protons and neutrons appear, with a gravitational-to-inertial ratio of about 0.99931 relative to the proton baseline. In absolute terms, photons and electrons behave like free protons and neutrons, which Darmos says feel about 1% less acceleration than iron. A photon's inertial mass grows with its energy, and so does its passive charge. That a photon can briefly make a quark–antiquark pair doesn't, on Darmos's account, violate energy conservation, because virtual pairs cannot move anything real (see the energy-conservation discussion below). Apparent and absolute acceleration coincide for passive charges.

The main test

Two centuries of big-G, re-read

The centrepiece is a meta-analysis of published G measurements, mostly from Gillies's 2014 survey and Kleinevoß's 2002 compilation plus other lists and papers. Darmos sorted each measurement by the composition of its source masses and its test masses, predicted each result from the composition-dependent values above, and asked whether the ordering of materials matches the ordering of the measured values. He predicts the highest G for iron, steel and copper, and the lowest for light elements and very heavy ones such as water, uranium and lead. Measured G is modelled as , a geometric mean of the individual values.

The headline result, and the rules behind it

The final rules include one value per person or team; all pre-1971 values excluded except Heyl's; any value with a standard deviation of ±0.01 or more excluded (poor air isolation and spring-scale experiments show up as unusually high values); the intermediate-range “fifth force” experiments excluded, all of which read unusually high; and non-Cavendish methods excluded. Darmos's actual choices go further than that list: it also drops Karagioz's series (on grounds it words strongly and this page doesn't repeat), Luther's later values, the Richman–Quinn servo-control method, Baldi's superconducting gravimeter and Schlamminger's tank value as read from a plotted graph, and it swaps Pontikis's 1972 paper for its average. Test-mass composition couldn't be established for four measurements (Dousse 1987, Speake 1983, Liu 1987, Oelfke 1984), so it was assumed to match the source. Swing dampers and heavy beams dwarf the test masses, so Darmos counts the composition of anything that moves with them.

By Darmos's own calculation, this leaves 27 data points after averaging same-material entries, forming 351 pairs, with concordant minus discordant pairs equal to 234 and five ties. That gives Kendall's τb ≈ 0.67 (0.6763 if ties are simply dropped), z ≈ 4.91, and p ≈ 4.5×10-7 — Darmos's “1 in 2,233,140.” Source-mass composition alone gives 1 in 824, and test-mass composition alone 1 in 43, weaker because beams and dampers dilute the test masses. Predicted values typically land within 0.01–0.1% of measured ones, but the predicted spread between common solids is only about 0.2% (steel 6.6742 to water 6.6617, by Darmos's own values), so agreement in absolute value says less than the ranking. How it was reached is set out in the two boxes at the end of this section.

Three historical cases Darmos leans on

Heyl (1926–30; 1942). Steel source masses with platinum, glass and gold test masses gave 6.667 ± 0.012 (platinum, best-of-day), 6.672 ± 0.004 (platinum, average), 6.671 ± 0.003 (glass) and 6.672 ± 0.004 (gold), against Darmos's predictions of 6.6712, 6.6720 and 6.6712 — all within error, and at least 99.94% in value. Heyl noticed considerable differences with the material, couldn't explain them, and repeated the Eötvös experiment for a null result, in Darmos's telling. Darmos concedes that Heyl's values agree in value but don't show the expected hierarchy, so on their own they “don't represent a strong case.” It also argues Heyl's early high readings, which fall as the balance settles, inflated the differences in his averages.

Pontikis (1971 and 1972). The 1971 paper reports material-specific values that Darmos says match its predictions, with lead–lead and mercury–mercury described as “phenomenal”; it puts the odds of a chance match at 0.25%. The 1972 paper reports only material-averaged values after an adjustment it doesn't specify, and Darmos uses the 1972 average, not its material values. A general note, here and later: where Darmos criticises named researchers or groups, this page reports what he points to and no more, and they may have had ordinary methodological reasons he doesn't consider. Darmos notes, for instance, that Taylor in 1973 discounted Heyl's and Pontikis's quoted deviations because they were statistical only.

Gundlach and Schlamminger. Darmos notes that the Eöt-Wash group's published Cavendish value used steel source masses, and that their equivalence-principle tests varied only the test-mass composition. Gundlach used uranium source masses with lead and copper test masses and reported no difference; Darmos notes that uranium's predicted G is very low and suggests that is why. Schlamminger's 2002 mercury-tank measurement is the only experiment in Darmos's data where changing test-mass composition (copper to tantalum) didn't seem to matter, and the tantalum results were later discarded by the authors as less accurate.

Why weighing the source masses matters

Darmos's argument is that scales measure weight, which depends on composition and location; mechanical balances measure inertial mass and cancel both, because the two pans form one system and the effective g on each side is a mix; electronic balances with magnetic force restoration cancel composition dependence but not elevation; and spring and absolute gravimeters can't swap test masses. It claims that Cavendish experiments whose source masses are weighed on mechanical balances can show composition effects, while a laboratory that weighs everything on a high-capacity electronic scale would hide them. The same reasoning, it says, explains why Eötvös-type balances give a near-null result. Some of Darmos's correspondents dispute this argument.

The analysis-flexibility caveat. Darmos dates every revision of this analysis, which is what makes the following visible. It began with a fixed “acceptable range” of G values (6.661–6.6745); on 11 Feb 2024 that range was abolished and replaced by excluding all pre-1971 values except Heyl's. Which of Heyl's repeated measurements to use (lowest, most frequent or average) was settled on 15 Feb 2024. Test-mass composition was added only on 26 Feb 2024; Darmos records that before 2024 it assumed test-mass composition had little effect. The p-values were “corrected” on 9 May 2026 and recomputed by exact enumeration on 12 May 2026. The further exclusions listed above (Karagioz, Luther's later values, the servo-control and superconducting-gravimeter methods, the tank value read from a graph) are choices of the same kind; each is defensible on its own terms, but together they belong in this caveat. None of this is hidden — but it means the headline odds describe the final analysis, not a test fixed in advance. An analysis adjusted as the data were examined is weaker evidence than a prediction made first, even when the final agreement is close. The same caution applies to the Eötvös and planetary-orbit sections below.

Darmos's odds assume the 27 data points behave like independent draws. Several share a laboratory, an apparatus or a material, and the underlying disagreement between modern G values is real and unresolved — mainstream metrologists generally attribute it to unrecognised systematic error. That does not show Darmos is wrong; it is why the ranking should be treated as suggestive and not as a settled discrepancy.

Experiments run by others

Freefall towers, neutrons, and a pendulum

Unlike a Cavendish balance, freefall and pendulum experiments compare test objects directly, so Darmos treats them as cleaner tests of composition-dependent gravity.

These are single experimenters or single research groups, none independently repeated, and Darmos himself calls the first of them “not very conclusive.” They were run by others; Darmos's own are kept separate in the next section.

A drop tower in Bremen

In 2004, Szász's experiment at the ZARM drop tower in Bremen put test masses of different materials on a shelf inside an aluminium capsule and dropped it 110 m, about 4.7 seconds of freefall (Darmos's own video timing gave 4.7 ± 0.05 s). As reported, lithium and lead lifted off the shelf, while iron and aluminium stayed — aluminium being the capsule's own material. Carbon lifted off with a delay, about 2 cm at impact, an effective 0.317 cm/s2 in Darmos's calculation. Beryllium and boron stayed down, which Darmos attributes to adhesion, since both have a large surface-to-mass ratio. Darmos's own comparison with the theory is rougher than that summary: lithium is predicted to lift about 31.96 cm but rose about 9 cm before the jar wall stopped it, and average accuracy across the three elements that lifted off is 52%.

Darmos lists the confounds: air was removed from the tower but not from the capsule; the lithium block hit the wall of its jar; residual magnetism is possible; and a correspondent suggested the two lift-offs came from the release impulse. He judges the result “not very conclusive” and recommends repeating the experiment without the capsule.

Neutron interferometers

Freefall tests need neutral particles, hence neutrons. Abele and Leeb's meta-analysis of neutron-interferometer gravity experiments, going back to Colella, Overhauser and Werner in 1975, found a consistent discrepancy of 0.8–1% between the prediction based on Newtonian gravity and the observation, with the predicted value always the larger — their quoted result is that the theoretical prediction of 59.2 ± 0.1 radians is 0.8% higher. Darmos's reading is that Gn is about 1% below GFe, “exactly as found.” It gives sign-test odds of 0.510, or 0.098%, for ten experiments all falling in one direction, and notes that since 1988 the discrepancies cluster around 1%. Darmos also notes that the paper's own authors attribute the discrepancy to a systematic error in the measurement equipment. The ten experiments share a technique and crystal interferometers, so they aren't independent draws either.

A pendulum in a vacuum tube

Giorgio Toro, an American-Italian experimenter whose self-published write-ups Darmos cites (one is titled “Musings of an amateur on inertia, gravity, light & time”), swung a pendulum in a vacuum tube for 16 days in 2023, with two swing directions each used twice. The bobs were iron (carbon steel), copper, aluminium and lead. Darmos's predicted ordering, Fe–Cu–Al–Pb, was observed exactly, which Darmos puts at p = 1/24, about 4%. A second round in August 2024 added Plexiglas, which the author had recommended for its very low predicted G; the observed order was Fe–Cu–Pb–Al–Plexiglas against a predicted Fe–Cu–Al–Pb–Plexiglas (τ = 0.8), giving p = 5/120, about 4.2%. Combined by Fisher's method, Darmos gives p = 0.0128, “1 in 78,” and an average agreement in value of 99.95% (99.96% without Plexiglas). Pendulums, unlike balances, respond to composition-dependent g. Darmos adds that Toro himself was not impressed by the size of his differences, because he didn't know what effect size to expect, and that the first experiment was finished before the author knew of it.

The differences between materials in Toro's data are of order 10-5 of the oscillation period or smaller, derived from the ratios Darmos lists — small enough that thermal and pressure drifts matter, though Darmos records that he tracked pressure, temperature, time and date. Nothing here has been independently repeated.

First-person, self-reported

The author's own experiments

Everything in this section was run and reported by Darmos himself — so far as he describes, unblinded and without an independent testing party. It's included because Darmos presents it as part of his case, and because two of the three experiments come with the author's own candid account of what went wrong. Treat it as description, not as data.

The 2020 Cavendish balance — a candid failure

In April and May 2020 the author built a torsion balance in an isolation container in a family factory, using water-filled buckets as source masses (the successful runs used four 20 kg buckets, 80 kg in all; a single two-bucket repeat used 40 kg) with 90 cm between the bucket centres, and reading angles with software. Early oscillation amplitudes were an order of magnitude too large. The author first blamed people walking nearby and micro-earthquakes; the real cause was insufficient air sealing, fixed with triple tape and a plastic sheet on 14–16 May 2020. The oscillation period was about 949 seconds (16 minutes), and the final reading, 6.523041×10-11 m3 kg-1 s-2, deviates significantly from both the mainstream value and the value this theory predicts, because the period couldn't be measured precisely from a half-hour video.

Darmos concludes the experiment is “useless for probing composition-dependent gravity,” because the source masses were weighed on an electronic scale, which cancels the effect (see the Cavendish section). Including that admission is an honesty point in Darmos's favour.

Freefall trials (June 2023 – January 2024)

The author used a Pasco freefall adapter with hollow “vending-machine” spheres filled with different materials, not in a vacuum. The first lead-versus-steel trials in June 2023 agreed with the prediction to 99.9887% (Darmos's entry of 18 June). A magnetic release followed: in the 21 November 2023 trials (20 pairs) the two materials came out at 0.6387 ± 0.0003 s and 0.6384 ± 0.0003 s, so the 0.0003 s difference was no larger than the uncertainty. From 3 December 2023 a 2.5 m drop height was used so that the standard deviations no longer overlapped; there, by Darmos's own account, the time difference was larger than expected. The headline pairing is lead against zinc: zinc fell faster in 17 of 19 trials, then in 38 of 42 overall, over a 1.762 m drop. The lead-filled ball weighed 59.757 g and the faster zinc-filled ball 59.160 g; Darmos notes that the faster one is the lighter, to rule out excess mass as the explanation.

Wood against zinc was messier. Initial trials showed the wood ball slower than predicted by 2–3 ms, which Darmos attributes to elasticity (a loose wood ball scrapes, and a rubber ball later behaved the same). Once the sphere was completely filled, the difference shrank to about 0.5 ms. The first clean run matched the formula for nutrient-rich wood (C6H12O6 plus calcium), and two later runs matched nutrient-poor dry wood (C6H12O6). Combining trials by Fisher's method (corrected 25 May 2026), Darmos gives “1 in 21.5 billion.”

Design choices Darmos explains: it uses the shortest rather than the average freefall time, because release delays can only lengthen a drop; single-photogate methods were rejected as insensitive, with two photogates later used to justify averaging; and zinc replaced steel to avoid magnetism at release, on a correspondent's suggestion. The confounds it records are air drag, adhesion, elasticity, dangling washers (traced as the reason standard deviations were small only with round steel spheres) and release impulse; early effect sizes were about 1–4 ms in standard deviation against differences of about 0.3 ms. Darmos treats the combined odds as decisive; the counts are real trial tallies, but every trial shared one apparatus, one person and several procedure changes, so the trials cannot be treated as independent draws.

Weighing scales (2025)

Darmos sorts weighing instruments into scales (weight; composition- and place-dependent), mechanical balances (mass; neither), and electronic force-restoration balances (composition cancels, elevation doesn't). He records that the author's earlier, 2024 version assumed all electronic balances were scales, and corrected this between 25 July and 23 August 2025. The author's own tests used a scale with 1-micronewton resolution, a 0.01 g triple-beam balance and, for fun, an iPhone 6s 3D-Touch screen with a metal spoon (0.81 g increments); Darmos says this wasn't yet precise enough to distinguish neighbouring elements, and that a proposed “material analyzer” would need balance-level resolution.

A famous null result

The Eötvös experiment, reread

Eötvös, Pekár and Fekete's 1922 torsion-balance experiment compared centrifugal (inertial) and gravitational forces on two masses of different composition hung from one beam. It has been cited for a century as evidence for the equivalence principle, and Einstein cited it too. Darmos calls it, ironically, the one experiment where no thought-out non-Newtonian theory would predict a directly detectable composition effect: the two sides form one system, since a balance can't tip one way unless the other goes the opposite way, so the effective g on each side is a mix.

In 1986, Fischbach's reanalysis found a correlation with baryon number per unit mass; Darmos cites Goldman's remark that Eötvös's quoted error was smaller than the individual uncertainties, “determined by the spread between the points.” Darmos reads the composition differences as not having overlapping error bars, and so as refuting the principle outright. Fischbach needed a free coupling constant, the “fifth force” of around 10-5 scale, to make the effect fit.

Darmos's own mechanism for a residual effect is that one test mass hangs on a string and so sits lower, closer to the Earth, than the standard mass on the other side; that unequal height makes the two compositions contribute unequally. The effect depends on suspension depth and is smaller than the measurement precision (20 July 2024), but small perturbations that grow over time amplify it linearly, which Darmos says needs very many trials — and Eötvös ran very many.

Darmos's reanalysis of ten material pairs (45 pair comparisons) has three steps, each with its own odds:

  1. Fischbach's method, on Eötvös's adjusted data: τ = 0.75, p = 0.000473, 1 in 2,114.
  2. The raw data, with the brass containers accounted for and the two sides still treated as independent: τ = 0.92, 1 in 333,333.
  3. Darmos's residual-chromogravity method, with the sides not independent and suspension depth included: a near-perfect ranking of all ten pairs: the observed τ is 0.966 because of ties, and Darmos's 1 in 3,628,800 (p = 1/10!) uses the next attainable value, τ = 1. Darmos calls this twice as strong in correlation as Fischbach's. A residual scale factor of about 100 remains, which Darmos explains through linear amplification; average value agreement is about 92%. The magnalium–platinum pair does not agree within its stated uncertainty. Darmos's adjustment (20 Sep 2024) applies a uniform rule — ±1×10-9 for copper pairs, ±2×10-9 for platinum pairs, as fractional differences in gravitational acceleration between the two materials — which changes only that pair, from ±1×10-9 to ±2×10-9; after that every pair agrees within error. A density check on Eötvös's 11.92 cm × 1.01 cm magnalium cylinder (25.402 g, or 2.66 g/cm3) suggests an alloy of about 95% aluminium, but Darmos rejects that reading and keeps its assumed composition.

The analysis-flexibility caveat from the Cavendish section applies here too: a uniform assumed uncertainty was applied after the fact (in effect widening only the magnalium–platinum pair), and an unexplained residual scale factor remains. The scale is fitted, so only the ranking test is free of tuning — and Darmos also argues that the effect should be about a million times larger than Eötvös measured, which is why the scale factor needs the suspension explanation.

Status: a reinterpretation of published data, not a new measurement.

A gap that may be a units problem

Neutron stars, black holes, and a mass gap

The rescaling below rests on Darmos's own binding-energy estimates and its claim that gravitational mass differs from inertial mass for compact objects. The argument is self-consistent within the framework and is not independent of it.

The idea: gravitational binding energy is negative mass-energy, so it reduces an object's inertial mass but not its gravitational mass. For a star collapsing to a neutron star (radius 1.5–2 Schwarzschild radii) that comes to about 17.5 ± 2.5% of the progenitor's mass, and for a black hole about 30%. Remnant masses inferred from gravity under general relativity, which assumes the two masses are equal, would then be too large by a factor that depends on composition and compactness. Darmos gives a neutron-star constant, GNu = 8.017303 ± 0.24×10-11 (about 1/0.83 times GFe), and says GNu and the black-hole constant are the first constants higher than steel's.

The heaviest known neutron star, PSR J0952−0607, has a general-relativity mass of 2.35 ± 0.17 solar masses. Recomputed with Darmos's masses, the observed gap between neutron stars and black holes shrinks from 0.95 to about 0.34 solar masses, which Darmos compares with the sample gap you'd expect between neighbouring black holes (average 0.325) and between neutron stars (average 0.052). He adds that a spacing as large as the original 0.96 solar masses occurs once among the eight black-hole spacings he lists, so the gap “was not too unusual” even before rescaling. The observed optical gap spans 2.35–3.3 solar masses.

Where Darmos challenges a standard identification

Binary pulsars: neutron stars or white dwarfs?

The Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar PSR B1913+16 — a double-neutron-star system — is regarded as some of the strongest evidence for general relativity; its discoverers won the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics. Its orbital decay matches general relativity's gravitational-wave prediction to within a fraction of a percent. What follows is Darmos's reinterpretation of that system and two similar ones; it contradicts the standard identification of these pulsars as neutron stars.

Darmos's starting point is that gravity here is carried by virtual gluons, not gravitons, so changes in a gravitational field must be accompanied by emission of real gluons. Orbital decay should then depend on inertial mass and total potential energy, and the smaller inertial masses that follow from binding energy and composition shift the predicted decay rate slightly. The ratio of this theory's prediction to general relativity's comes out at about 0.9992 (0.99917 ± 0.0042; 0.99920 ± 0.0042 with the white-dwarf binding energy). The predicted departure from general relativity, about 0.0008, is far smaller than that uncertainty, while the observed 2016 ratio is 0.9983 ± 0.0016, so agreement within the margins is easy to reach.

On what the pulsars are: using general-relativity masses, PSR B1913+16 has a pulsar of 1.438 ± 0.001 solar masses and a companion of 1.390 ± 0.001 (Weisberg and Huang, 2016). Both sit below the Chandrasekhar limit — about 1.445 solar masses times (2/μe)2, where μe is 2 for helium, carbon or oxygen and 56/26 for iron, and about 1.38–1.39 once general relativity is applied. So, per Darmos, they can't be neutron stars under his own framework, whose masses come out smaller still, and are likelier a white-dwarf pulsar plus an ordinary white dwarf. Rapid rotation (over 16 revolutions per second) could raise the limit, and Darmos notes that mass shedding at formation (Boshkayev 2012) doesn't apply to spin-up by accretion after collapse. He gives the reason for the literature's preference for neutron stars — they explain the magnetic field and spin more easily — and notes that no spectroscopy is possible for pulsars, so their chemical composition is unknown. He also concedes that white-dwarf-pulsar physics requires a lot of additional research.

This is Darmos reclassifying a well-studied class of objects, and it goes further than the reinterpretations of data elsewhere on this page. The identification of these systems as neutron stars is standard, and Darmos's alternative is a proposal, not an observation.

Three checks in the solar system

Mercury, the planets, and Kepler's third law

Mercury's perihelion

Darmos treats the other planets as concentric rings around the Sun, which he argues is reasonable because Mercury's perihelion advances so slowly that the planets' positions average out over the long time it takes to circle the Sun. He uses composition-dependent G-values and inertial masses for each planet, a series expansion in the ratios of orbital radii (36 terms give the digits it displays), and a correction factor of 0.9668 to convert the ring-model result to the exact elliptical Newtonian one.

Three contributions stay the same as in general relativity: the relativistic term (42.9799″ per century), Lense–Thirring (−0.0020) and solar oblateness (0.0286). This theory changes only the Newtonian planetary-perturbation term, 531.853″ per century against the exact Newtonian 532.3035, a difference of about 0.45″. The totals are 574.8594 ± 0.0015 for this theory, 575.31 ± 0.0015 for general relativity, and a modern observed value of 574.7 ± 0.45. By Darmos's calculation the first agrees within error and to 99.97% in value, the second to 99.89% and not within error (both at strict one-sigma).

The other planets

Older data (Newcomb 1895; Duncombe 1956) give an anomalous precession of 8.4 ± 4.8″ per century for Venus and 5.0 ± 1.2 for Earth, against general relativity's 8.61, 3.84 and 1.35 for Venus, Earth and Mars. For the inner planets' total precession, Darmos's average agreement is 99.90% for this theory against 99.75% for general relativity (7 April 2025). He concedes that Earth's high total rate flatters the total-precession agreement, and that on the anomalous part this theory is most precise for Mercury and Venus, then Earth and Mars.

Darmos says modern data can't be used for the inner planets, because published ephemeris analyses (it cites Anderson 1992 and Ohanian & Ruffini 1994, and describes current fitting practice the same way) fit a single shared parameter to all planets, so any planet-specific composition effect would be absorbed. For the outer planets there is real telemetry, but ephemerides are refit against Newtonian models, which propagates any anomaly to neighbouring planets. Saturn (Pitjeva's anomaly, around 2006) and Jupiter (the INPOP10a discrepancy, 2011) are read as composition-dependent reductions, recalculated on 29 and 31 March 2025 respectively; Darmos acknowledges Pitjeva's and Iorio's stringent constraints, a few milliarcseconds per century.

Kepler's third law and the "Kepler ratio"

Kepler's third law, that the square of an orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, holds only if the equivalence principle does. Under this theory each planet should have a slightly different Kepler ratio, depending on composition. Modern semi-major axes for the inner planets are derived from orbital periods using Kepler's law, not measured, so they look uniform by construction. The cleanest uncorrupted data, Darmos argues, are Tycho Brahe's measurements as Kepler used them (1617/1619). The predicted ordering is Mercury–Earth–Venus–Mars–Saturn–Jupiter, and the observed ordering matches, with all planets but Saturn within error.

Three loose ends

What this does to relativity

The first two items are about how to interpret relativity's mathematics, and the third about a particular energy-conservation puzzle; none is a new laboratory test.

Chronology protection

General relativity admits closed timelike curves, paths that loop back in time: Flamm's 1916 wormhole solution, Gödel's rotating universe (1949), and Thorne's scheme of charging one wormhole mouth, dragging it near light speed with a magnet and so creating a time difference. The standard objections are that a wormhole needs exotic matter to stay open; Hawking's argument that vacuum fluctuations looping through would grow and destroy it; Wheeler and Fuller's 1962 result that a wormhole pinches off before light can cross; and that white holes collapse in fractions of a second.

Darmos adds a few of its own. A Kerr black hole's ring-singularity “exit” would give two black holes from one, violating energy conservation. The mass inflation inside a black hole (Israel and Poisson, 1990) reaches about 1057 times the mass of the universe as seen from within, so if a white-hole exit opened even briefly it would expose that to the outside, which Darmos says would destroy the universe. In a closed universe, black-hole interiors would have to reach the far side (a 2003 worry, which also undercuts Lee Smolin's cosmic-evolution idea in closed universes). And Thorne's own many-worlds escape from paradoxes doesn't stop the paradox arising in the original spacetime; the related many-worlds “immortality” idea is criticised on the Similar-Worlds Interpretation's page.

On this framework the problem doesn't arise, because curvature is a granular density difference, not a property of spacetime. Kip Thorne's 1989 membrane paradigm is the nearest mainstream cousin — rulers and clocks change, with no spacetime and no curvature, and time really stops at the Schwarzschild radius — though Darmos says Thorne treated it as merely a useful model. In the Similar-Worlds picture, time travel would mean switching between spacetimes, leaving the community of entangled observers, which Darmos says would simply lead to nonexistence. He also charges that the mainstream takes a positivist view of general relativity's inconsistencies; this page doesn't take that further.

Absolute space and “correlation-like” phenomena

Darmos argues that “everything is relative” is a philosophy layered on relativity's equations, not something they imply. Operationally, length contraction and time dilation can equally be described by a varying speed of light, which Einstein himself often did, and which doesn't require a spacetime block. In the twin paradox, it is the travelling twin who slows during acceleration. The two-astronauts argument works only if they have no memory or instruments; a dipole in the cosmic microwave background reveals motion, though Sengal showed other radiation dipoles imply different velocities and directions, so Darmos doubts a purely local-motion reading (see the CMB material on the Entropic Expansion page).

It matters because quantum mechanics needs collapse to be simultaneous across space, and the Similar-Worlds picture needs one set of temporarily indistinguishable worlds at a time; both want a preferred frame. Telepathic information is, in Darmos's phrase, correlation-like rather than signal-like, and so, like entanglement, isn't bound by the speed limit — see the Conscious Set Theory page, which carries a much stronger caveat on that claim.

There is a tension Darmos doesn't fully resolve. The Similar-Worlds page says only each observer's past light cone is real, while this section asks for an absolute frame. The reconciliation Darmos offers, dated 24 November 2020, is that the frame in which an entanglement was established fixes that entanglement's “now-slice.” A dated reversal sits nearby: on 14 November 2020 Darmos proposed a speed-dependent deformation of the wavefunction, and by 21 November it had dropped that, in favour of speed determining how out of phase spin entanglement is.

Conservation of gravitational energy

Since the universe isn't a closed system in this framework — it has no border, the cosmic microwave background's energy is continually lost to expansion, and a universe with entropic expansion can't collapse (see the Entropic Expansion page) — Darmos says the first and second laws apply to closed systems only.

A correspondent of Darmos's, a physicist, put a challenge: if a photon makes a quark–antiquark pair (a meson) that reverts to a photon, and photons exert no gravity while mesons do, then gravitational energy appears and vanishes. Darmos's answer is that a meson would have to live long enough for its gravity to be measured. With a potential energy for a meson pair at 0.5 fm of −2.4624×10-49 J, the measurement time is , about 2.14×1014 seconds — millions of years. The longest-lived meson, the neutral long-lived kaon, lives 5.116×10-8 seconds, so no meson lives long enough to violate conservation measurably. And because the gravitational charge photons pick up from virtual pairs is passive, no real particle is moved either. In this framework all mesons share the same potential energy, since only quark counts enter, unlike in general relativity.

Proposals, not experiments

Blocking, cancelling and altering gravity

Nothing in this section has been built or tested; it is Darmos's reasoning, at thought-experiment level, about what his own framework would allow. In mainstream physics, gravity can't be blocked or shielded; that's a consequence of gravity being universal and, in general relativity, a property of spacetime.

Why exotic matter doesn't help, and why blocking might

In general relativity, anti-gravity needs exotic, negative-mass matter. In this framework exotic matter has negative inertial mass but positive gravitational mass — self-repelling but still attracted to ordinary matter — and all of it is locked in ordinary-matter shells inside supermassive black holes (see the Entropic Expansion page), so it is inaccessible. Eric Laithwaite's 1983 “lighter spinning wheel” Darmos treats as an illusion, since the spin cancels the force needed to keep the bar horizontal.

What might work instead is shielding. With gluons rather than gravitons mediating gravity, gravity has a preferred wavelength, the diameter of a quark, and in principle a medium in which every straight path hits a nucleus could block it, as in a neutron star. Darmos supposes a neutron star would block gravity from objects behind it: virtual gluons entering are scattered back out the side they entered, and those emitted inside exit on the nearest side, so the total gluon number, and with it energy conservation, is unchanged. His own estimate is that even the densest Earth solids scatter each gluon many times but let every gluon through eventually, so they would only affect the speed of gravity, not its strength; blocking is all or nothing. A test would be to remove a source quickly and time how long a test mass takes to notice.

A proposed lattice would stack layers of a crystal, each displaced by one quark diameter so that every straight line ends at a nucleus. Darmos's own assessment is that blocking a significant number of gluons this way is unlikely and unguaranteed, though even a slight weight reduction would be remarkable. Electrogravity is easier to screen — Faraday cages and Casimir plates block virtual photons readily, and its range is only about 40 m (14 August 2020) — but the resulting “gravity plates” would be extremely weak: Darmos puts the weight reduction for a 40 m radius at 0.000031927% and says he will run the test when he has the equipment. He credits an artificial-gravity idea based on quark–gluon plasma to a collaborator (27 July 2020).

Local homogeneity: when gravity vanishes

In general relativity, a small enough region is “locally flat” and tidal forces vanish. The analogue here is density differences in granular space, which live in space alone, since time isn't a dimension in this framework. Gravity disappears when the spacing of field lines varies by less than a Planck length across an object's wavefunction. There are two routes: confine the wavefunction, so that a long narrow gap flattens it, or move far enough from the source that the region of homogeneity outgrows the object. Field strength and local homogeneity are independent — strength is how many field lines there are, homogeneity is the spacing difference at the region's borders. Darmos criticises Einstein's elevator on these grounds: an accelerating rocket and a gravitational field differ in tidal forces (head versus feet, shoulders converging), and shrinking the elevator to hide that removes gravity itself, too.

Darmos adds three dated notes that qualify this. On 27 October 2020 it added that gravity doesn't fall off with distance for a confined wavefunction, and does so only for objects of one particular size. On 2 November 2020 it noted that the argument about dropping atoms doesn't depend on its “wavefunction-mediated gravity” hypothesis. And on 17 November 2020 it declared an earlier neutron-star argument in this discussion invalid.

The engineering below is further still from anything tested: it proposes hardware for an effect that needs a plasma at roughly three billion kelvin, and none of it has been built.

Changing gravity per kilogram: in-frame inertial mass

Gravity per kilogram can be altered by adding inertial mass without adding quarks: extra electrons (impractical, since they add a tiny mass and a huge charge) or the kinetic or thermal energy of components within the frame, not the ship's motion as a whole, which cancels, on the same principle as the laws section. The ratio γmX in GX grows while gravitational mass doesn't. Inflating a large mass this way would need a hot plasma or relativistic gas, but the reactors and accelerators involved would outweigh the plasma. Darmos sketches concentric proton and electron storage rings and hollow-core-fibre wakefield accelerators in ring shapes, and lists collisions, heating, laser attenuation and sustaining the ring geometry as problems; xenon, which is bright when accelerated, is noted as practical, radon as not. For the ~1% composition differences Darmos's realistic use is gravity sensors, measuring an unknown object's mass and gravity to identify its material; he calls those differences far too small for any gravity-altering technology. For in-frame mass inflation he is more optimistic: he puts a doubling of inertial mass at a plasma moving at about 86% of light speed (roughly 3 billion K), calls a xenon-filled hollow fibre “ideal conditions,” and goes on to a hoverboard and artificial gravity. He also concedes that take-off would save no energy, because the force needed to hold the craft up is unchanged. Darmos's own xenon-versus-fibre mass comparison contains a unit slip (xenon is about 0.0059 g/cm3); a rough recalculation for this guide still has the gas outweighing a 0.001 mm shell, by about four times.

Status: proposed, never built or tested.
