An independent theory of everything, in progress since 2003

Elementary spaces, built into a theory of everything

"The Complete Guide to Quantum Gravity" is physicist Sky Darmos's independent framework linking quantum mechanics, cosmology, and consciousness — six interlocking theories, built patiently over two decades, on one unifying idea: that space itself is made of overlapping spherical surfaces. It's an ambitious attempt to answer questions the field still hasn't closed, and this guide is here to help you follow the reasoning.

Explore the six theories See what it sets out to solve
A note on what this is. This site is a guide to one physicist's independent work, not a summary of settled science. Sky Darmos has spent over twenty years building this framework largely on his own, outside any university or peer-review process, and he describes it as still evolving. Some of what he proposes reaches well beyond where mainstream cosmology and particle physics currently stand — a universe far older than 13.8 billion years, gravity that varies slightly with what you're weighing, a role for psychokinesis and telepathy that goes further than even parapsychology's own literature usually does. Those departures are part of what makes the work distinctive, and we point them out where they come up so you can weigh them yourself. The aim of this guide is to walk through Darmos's reasoning on its own terms, the way he built it.

What the framework sets out to address

Thirty long-standing problems

Darmos's title page lists thirty problems in physics and cosmology, and offers a proposed resolution for each — presented below as one line apiece. That a single framework takes on this many open questions at once is a large part of its appeal. These are Darmos's own claims, offered as his contribution to open questions rather than as settled fact, grouped here by field. A few are sketched more lightly than the rest: he states the Hubble tension and the M–sigma relation briefly in a summary table without much worked argument, gathers the spin interpretation and CMB fluctuations from scattered passages rather than one dedicated section, and gives quasicrystals a single paragraph. The full index gives every problem its chapter and how much this site covers.

Quantum foundations The measurement problem Measurement is built into the wavefunction itself — it's the collective knowledge of every conscious observer. See Similar-Worlds Interpretation.
Quantum foundations The Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox Entangled spins are already antipodal in every one of the temporarily indistinguishable worlds we occupy — no signal needs to travel. See SWI.
Cosmology The accelerated expansion problem Black hole entropy grows with the square of mass, and expansion exists to keep entropy density constant — acceleration falls out for free. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The Hubble tension There's no single expansion rate to disagree about — the rate itself depends on local entropy, so different methods were never measuring the same thing. Darmos states this briefly, with little worked argument. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The missing matter problem (dark matter) In a universe this old, roughly 90% of matter has simply burned out — ordinary stellar remnants, not an undiscovered particle. See Entropic Expansion.
Gravity The vacuum catastrophe Gravity is always local, never global, so the enormous energy of the vacuum has nothing to pull against and can't tear the universe apart. See Space Particle Dualism.
Gravity The black hole information paradox No true event horizon ever forms, and photons carry no gravity to lose — so there's nothing for Hawking radiation to carry away. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology Background dependence Space isn't a stage matter sits on — every particle carries its own quantum of space, so geometry and matter are the same substance. See Space Particle Dualism.
Cosmology The flatness problem In a borderless universe, gravity pulling from every direction cancels out — flatness needs no fine-tuning, and inflation becomes unnecessary. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The horizon problem The early universe was never a special low-entropy state — entropy density has always been the same constant it is today. See Entropic Expansion.
Gravity Chronology protection Space can't bend into a wormhole loop, because nothing ever truly bends — granular density differences stand in for curvature, and no true horizon forms. See Chromogravity.
Gravity The singularity problem There's no infinitely curved starting point: the "Big Bang" is a low-density border, and a perfectly uniform early universe would curve nothing however dense it was. For black holes, Darmos's summary answer is that infinite time dilation freezes a collapsing star before a singularity or true horizon forms, tied to a preferred frame he says quantum mechanics needs. See Entropic Expansion.
Particle physics The hierarchy problem Gravity looks vanishingly weak because it's diluted by the sheer density of the quantum vacuum's virtual particles — not because it's a separate, mismatched force. See Space Particle Dualism.
Consciousness The mind–body problem Consciousness is a relation between experiences, not a substance in the brain — closer to a Platonic set than to a signal. See Conscious Set Theory.
Quantum foundations The causality problem Quantum randomness is replaced with non-computable determinism — free, meaningful choice rather than dice, following from Gödel's incompleteness theorem. See SWI.
Quantum foundations The quantum spin interpretation problem There's no single dedicated section for this one; the answer is assembled from several passages. Massless particles have one elementary-space component and massive fermions two merged ones (a two-component spinor), a photon's spin is its chirality, and merged elementary spaces generate a fresh spin ½. The muon and tauon spin-energy calculation on the Charge Mass Dualism page builds on the last of these.
Quantum foundations Quasicrystals Five-fold quasicrystals can't grow by classical local rules, so — following Penrose — Darmos suggests they grow in superposition and settle when the wavefunction collapses, offering this as support for consciousness-caused collapse and a possible test of collapse timing. He gives it a single paragraph. See SWI.
Particle physics The generations of matter Muons and tauons are merged elementary spaces of electrons — their masses fall out of how many electron-spaces combine. See Charge Mass Dualism.
Particle physics The Koide formula The merged-electron model reproduces the Koide ratio to 99.85% — reached through a sequence of correction terms added one at a time, which is worth keeping in mind alongside the headline number. See Charge Mass Dualism.
Particle physics The origin of mass Mass is a particle's own field energy, not an added property: an electron's self-energy matches its measured mass to seven figures, and heavier particles are built by merging elementary spaces — masses the Higgs mechanism leaves as free parameters. See Charge Mass Dualism.
Cosmology The M–sigma relation Every supermassive black hole grew from a primordial black hole on the same clock as its host galaxy, so the two were never going to be independent. Darmos doesn't work through a full derivation of the relation itself. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The arrow of time The universe's entropy density is fixed at a constant, maintained by expansion — time has a direction because expansion does. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology CMB anomalies An emergent universe, retro-causally shaped by observation on Earth, would leave exactly this kind of asymmetry stamped on the microwave background. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology Non-Copernican structures Concentric-shell-like structure in galaxy distributions is now attributed to baryon acoustic oscillations in an emergent universe; an earlier reading tied to observation centred on Earth is kept only for the north–south asymmetry. The BAO fit uses a tuned initial temperature. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology Fluctuations in the CMB Small fluctuations trace to primordial black holes formed when an exotic-matter crystal collapsed; without them there is no expansion to stretch and amplify small random fluctuations. Darmos says his future-observation principle doesn't cover these. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The missing local expansion Expansion is entropic and non-homogeneous, so it's far too small to detect near a dense, low-entropy region like our own galaxy. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The supernova luminosity problem Intergalactic distances are around three times larger than assumed, which restores the inverse-square law and explains why remote supernovae looked dim — a result that depends heavily on which distance is used as the baseline. See Entropic Expansion.
Cosmology The quasar redshift problem The same larger-distance relationship is applied to quasars: the most distant one charted on the Entropic Expansion page (z = 7.54) comes out near 299 billion light-years instead of 13. See Entropic Expansion.
Gravity The trouble with big-G Newton's constant was never universal — measured gravity depends on the chemical composition of whatever you're weighing. See Chromogravity.
Gravity The neutron star–black hole mass gap The gap shrinks to about a third of its size once masses are recalculated from quark count instead of assuming the equivalence principle. See Chromogravity.

The framework, piece by piece

Six theories, one account of space, matter, and mind

Darmos developed each of these on its own before weaving them into a single framework, and what makes the project distinctive is how tightly they lock together. They build on one another roughly in this order — open any one below for the core idea, told in plain language.

2003
Similar-Worlds Interpretation

We exist in every world we can't tell apart

Instead of one observer collapsing one reality, consciousness is a moving "cloud" across every version of the universe that currently looks the same. Each measurement shrinks the cloud. Entanglement stops being spooky: paired particles simply keep opposite spins in every one of the shared worlds, with nothing needing to travel between them.

Read the core idea

Standard quantum mechanics treats a superposition as a mystery of a single particle being "everywhere at once." Darmos offers a clearer picture: the whole universe exists as a cloud of near-identical states in phase space, and our consciousness sits inside all of them simultaneously, for as long as they stay indistinguishable. A measurement doesn't choose between hidden values — it shrinks the set of worlds that count as "us." He pairs the idea with a concrete quantum-eraser experiment designed to test it.

This is the interpretive foundation the rest of the framework sits on: Space Particle Dualism explains why those states form wave-like probability patterns, and Conscious Set Theory explains what "consciousness" is doing the observing.

2005
Space Particle Dualism

Space is made of overlapping spheres

Every particle carries its own "elementary space" — a two-dimensional spherical surface whose size scales with its mass-energy. Billions of overlapping elementary spaces, mostly from virtual particles in the vacuum, weave together into the three-dimensional space we experience. Gravity becomes a density difference in this granular fabric, not curved spacetime.

Read the core idea

Point particles are notoriously hard to reconcile with general relativity's curved geometry. Darmos instead gives every particle an extended, complex-valued spherical surface. Its radius formula has the shape of a black hole's horizon radius, scaled to the strength of electromagnetism rather than gravity — though a later chapter cautions against picturing these surfaces as tiny black holes. More mass nearby means more overlapping spheres, which means more paths lead toward it — and that surplus of paths is what we experience as gravitational attraction.

The same oscillating, complex-valued surfaces also give particles their wave-like probability patterns, which is how this connects back to the Similar-Worlds Interpretation and eventually revises the Born rule itself.

2023
Charge Mass Dualism

Mass is a side effect of charge

An electron's mass turns out to match the energy stored in its own electromagnetic field almost exactly — mass isn't fundamental, it's what charge looks like from a distance. Extending the same logic to the strong force predicts the muon and tauon masses from merged electron elementary spaces, matching measured values to better than 99.9%.

Read the core idea

The idea traces back to 1881 and J.J. Thomson, who first suggested that a charged particle's inertia comes from the energy of its own field. Darmos revives that "electromagnetic mass" idea and extends it: if two or three electron-charges merge within one classical electron radius — emitting one or two positrons to conserve charge — the result is, respectively, a muon or a tauon.

Because gravity is itself a side effect of charge in this framework (see Chromogravity), this chapter is really the missing link between particle masses and gravity's own strength.

2013
Entropic Expansion Cosmology

The universe expands to keep entropy constant

Black holes are where almost all of the universe's entropy lives, and their entropy grows with the square of their mass. If overall entropy density has to stay constant, space itself has to expand — faster and faster, exactly tracking that square-law growth. No dark energy required, and the "Big Bang" becomes a low-density border rather than a singular explosion.

Read the core idea

Conventional cosmology treats the early universe as needing a special, fine-tuned low-entropy starting point. Here, the entropy density of the universe never changes — the "Big Bang" is just the low-density, high-temperature border where black holes were smallest, not a moment when the laws were different.

Working the numbers back from today's entropy density gives a post-decoupling age of roughly 42 trillion years, far older than the standard 13.8 billion — a bold claim that Darmos backs with his own analysis of white dwarf cooling times, supernova brightness, and galaxy redshift surveys, and which is the most consequential departure from mainstream cosmology in the framework.

2004
Conscious Set Theory

A feeling is a set, not a substance

Borrowing from Gottlob Frege's set-theoretic definition of numbers, this theory defines a feeling — like the warmth of red, or the meaning of a word — as the set of every experience anyone has ever had of it. That shared, nonlocal "world of experience" is also where Darmos locates telepathy, psychokinesis, and remote viewing, treating them as measurable consequences of a mind that isn't confined to one brain. It's the most speculative reach of the whole framework, and worth reading with that in mind.

Read the core idea

Frege defined the number "three" as the set of every group of three things. Conscious Set Theory applies the same move to qualia: the feeling of seeing red is the set of every experience of red anyone has ever had, not a signal sitting in one skull. Because these sets aren't tied to a single nervous system, Darmos treats synchronicity, telepathy, and psychokinesis as ordinary — if statistically subtle — consequences of a shared, nonlocal "world of experience."

This is the part of the framework furthest from mainstream science on its own terms: Darmos leans here on parapsychology research (particularly Dean Radin's) that most physicists, and many parapsychologists, consider unresolved or contested. It's a striking, imaginative extension of the framework — and one to read with eyes open.

2017
Chromogravity & Electrogravity

Gravity depends on what you're weighing

If gravity is a side effect of charge, then it should scale with quark count (chromogravity) rather than mass, plus a much shorter-range contribution from electric charge (electrogravity). That means Newton's "constant" isn't constant — it should read slightly differently for iron, water, and lead. Darmos tests this directly against a century of Cavendish, pendulum, and free-fall experiments.

Read the core idea

Nuclear binding energy means a kilogram of iron and a kilogram of water contain slightly different numbers of protons and neutrons. If gravity tracks quark count rather than mass directly, every element should have its own gravitational constant, differing by roughly 0.1% among common metals and about 1% between iron and hydrogen.

That's a testable, falsifiable claim, and Darmos's later chapters are essentially a data project: reanalyzing historical measurements of big-G, the Eötvös experiment, pendulum trials, and free-fall drops, arguing that the scatter researchers usually treat as measurement error actually tracks chemical composition. It's a real departure from the equivalence principle — one of general relativity's most tested foundations — so it's worth reading his own evidence pages with that scale of claim in mind.

Slow down for these five

Ideas worth a plain-language pass

A handful of concepts do most of the work across all six theories. Open any of these for the short version, without the equations.

What is a superposition, here?

Not "everywhere at once" — a cloud of near-identical worlds.

Read the explanation

In most descriptions of quantum mechanics, a superposition is treated as a single particle somehow occupying multiple states at once, which is famously hard to picture. Darmos offers a more intuitive picture: think of every possible state of the entire universe as a point in an enormous space of possibilities. A superposition is a small cloud of nearby points — worlds so similar that nothing yet distinguishes them.

Consciousness sits inside every world in that cloud simultaneously. A measurement doesn't pick a hidden value out of thin air; it shrinks the cloud down until only the worlds consistent with what you just observed remain.

What is granular space?

Space built from overlapping spherical surfaces, not a smooth backdrop.

Read the explanation

Every particle — mostly the virtual particles constantly appearing and vanishing in the vacuum — carries its own small, two-dimensional spherical surface, sized to its energy. Pack enough of these "elementary spaces" together and their overlaps weave into the smooth, three-dimensional space we move through.

Near a massive object, more of these surfaces pile up, which means more possible paths lead toward it. That surplus of paths is what shows up, at everyday scales, as gravitational pull — geometry and matter turn out to be the same stuff.

How does black hole entropy drive expansion?

Entropy grows as mass², so the universe has to keep pace.

Read the explanation

A black hole's entropy is proportional to the square of its mass, so as black holes across the universe slowly grow, total entropy grows even faster. The proposal is that the average entropy density of the universe never changes — it's held constant by the expansion of space itself.

Because entropy grows with the square of mass, the expansion needed to keep density constant also has to accelerate — matching the observed acceleration without needing to invoke a separate dark energy.

Consciousness as a relation, not a substance

A feeling is the set of every experience of it, not a signal in a brain.

Read the explanation

Borrowing Gottlob Frege's trick for defining numbers — "three" is the set of every group of three things — this theory defines a feeling the same way. The warmth of the color red isn't stored anywhere; it's the set of every experience of red that has ever happened, which is why it can feel the same to two different people.

That "world of experience" isn't confined to one skull, which is the theoretical basis Darmos uses for treating telepathy and psychokinesis as measurable, if statistically weak, phenomena rather than impossibilities. That step goes beyond what mainstream science accepts, and is noted as such on the theory's own page.

Composition-dependent gravity

A kilogram of lead and a kilogram of water shouldn't fall quite alike.

Read the explanation

If gravity tracks the number of quarks in an object rather than its mass, then two objects of identical mass but different chemical makeup — say, iron versus water — should experience very slightly different gravitational pull, because nuclear binding energy means they don't contain quite the same number of protons and neutrons per kilogram.

This is a genuine departure from Einstein's equivalence principle, one of general relativity's most tested foundations — worth knowing going in. It's also what makes the idea testable: Darmos's later chapters reanalyze historical big-G measurements, pendulum trials, and free-fall drops looking for exactly this signature.

About the author

Sky Darmos

Born on an olive plantation in Greece to a Greek father and a German mother, Darmos traces the framework's first ideas to a solar-system drawing in the sand on a New Year's night, and to two specific journeys: a train platform in Pforzheim, Germany, in 2004, and a car ride to a beach near Skala, Greece, in 2005.

Between 2007 and 2012 the work paused entirely for an unrelated six-year detour into etymology — a comparative study of German and Chinese cognates — before returning to physics in Macao in 2012. He has revised the manuscript continuously since a first short draft in 2014, moving through what he calls six phases: conceptual, computational, and then four rounds of gathering evidence, from cosmology through gravity, particle physics, and astrophysics. That habit of dating and revising his own work in the open is one of its most distinctive features.

The manuscript is dedicated to Sir Roger Penrose, cited throughout as Darmos's primary influence, alongside John Archibald Wheeler, Paul Davies, and several independent gravity researchers whose experimental data he reanalyzes directly.
