Elementary spaces, built into a theory of everything

"The Complete Guide to Quantum Gravity" is physicist Sky Darmos's independent framework linking quantum mechanics, cosmology, and consciousness — six interlocking theories built over two decades, on the claim that space itself is made of overlapping spherical surfaces.

Explore the six theories See what it claims to solve
A note on what this is. This site explains a theory, not a consensus. The book is self-published, independent research, built up over twenty years of one person's work, and it has not been peer-reviewed. Several of its central claims — a universe roughly 42 trillion years old, gravity that depends on chemical composition, and literal psychokinesis and telepathy — contradict the standard model of cosmology and particle physics, and reach further than even parapsychology's own contested literature does. What follows is a guide to one researcher's ambitious, internally consistent attempt at a unified theory, presented on its own terms.

The framework, piece by piece

Six theories, one account of space, matter, and mind

Each idea was developed on its own, then folded into a single framework. They build on each other roughly in this order — open any one for the core idea in plain language.

2003
Similar-Worlds Interpretation

We exist in every world we can't tell apart

Instead of one observer collapsing one reality, consciousness is a moving "cloud" across every version of the universe that currently looks the same. Each measurement shrinks the cloud. Entanglement stops being spooky: paired particles simply keep opposite spins in every one of the shared worlds, with nothing needing to travel between them.

Read the core idea

Standard quantum mechanics treats a superposition as a mystery of a single particle being "everywhere at once." SWI reframes it: the whole universe exists as a cloud of near-identical states in phase space, and our consciousness sits inside all of them simultaneously, for as long as they stay indistinguishable. A measurement doesn't choose between hidden values — it shrinks the set of worlds that count as "us."

This is the interpretive foundation the rest of the framework sits on: Space Particle Dualism explains why those states form wave-like probability patterns, and Conscious Set Theory explains what "consciousness" is doing the observing.

2005
Space Particle Dualism

Space is made of overlapping spheres

Every particle carries its own "elementary space" — a two-dimensional spherical surface whose size scales with its mass-energy. Billions of overlapping elementary spaces, mostly from virtual particles in the vacuum, weave together into the three-dimensional space we experience. Gravity becomes a density difference in this granular fabric, not curved spacetime.

Read the core idea

Point particles are notoriously hard to reconcile with general relativity's curved geometry. SPD instead gives every particle an extended, complex-valued spherical surface. Its radius formula has the shape of a black hole's horizon radius, scaled to the strength of electromagnetism rather than gravity — though a later chapter cautions against picturing these surfaces as tiny black holes. More mass nearby means more overlapping spheres, which means more paths lead toward it — and that surplus of paths is what we experience as gravitational attraction.

The same oscillating, complex-valued surfaces also give particles their wave-like probability patterns, which is how SPD connects back to the Similar-Worlds Interpretation and eventually revises the Born rule itself.

2023
Charge Mass Dualism

Mass is a side effect of charge

An electron's mass turns out to match the energy stored in its own electromagnetic field almost exactly — mass isn't fundamental, it's what charge looks like from a distance. Extending the same logic to the strong force predicts the muon and tauon masses from merged electron elementary spaces, matching measured values to better than 99.9%.

Read the core idea

The idea traces back to 1881 and J.J. Thomson, who first suggested that a charged particle's inertia comes from the energy of its own field. Charge Mass Dualism revives that "electromagnetic mass" idea and extends it: if two or three electron-charges merge within one classical electron radius — emitting one or two positrons to conserve charge — the result is, respectively, a muon or a tauon.

Because gravity is itself a side effect of charge in this framework (see Chromogravity), this chapter is really the missing link between particle masses and gravity's own strength.

2013
Entropic Expansion Cosmology

The universe expands to keep entropy constant

Black holes are where almost all of the universe's entropy lives, and their entropy grows with the square of their mass. If overall entropy density has to stay constant, space itself has to expand — faster and faster, exactly tracking that square-law growth. No dark energy required, and the "Big Bang" becomes a low-density border rather than a singular explosion.

Read the core idea

Conventional cosmology treats the early universe as needing a special, fine-tuned low-entropy starting point. Here, the entropy density of the universe never changes — the "Big Bang" is just the low-density, high-temperature border where black holes were smallest, not a moment when the laws were different.

Working the numbers back from today's entropy density gives a post-decoupling age of roughly 42 trillion years, far older than the standard 13.8 billion — a claim the book supports with its own analysis of white dwarf cooling times, supernova brightness, and galaxy redshift surveys.

2004
Conscious Set Theory

A feeling is a set, not a substance

Borrowing from Gottlob Frege's set-theoretic definition of numbers, this theory defines a feeling — like the warmth of red, or the meaning of a word — as the set of every experience anyone has ever had of it. That shared, nonlocal "world of experience" is also where the book locates telepathy, psychokinesis, and remote viewing, treating them as measurable consequences of a mind that isn't confined to one brain.

Read the core idea

Frege defined the number "three" as the set of every group of three things. Conscious Set Theory applies the same move to qualia: the feeling of seeing red is the set of every experience of red anyone has ever had, not a signal sitting in one skull. Because these sets aren't tied to a single nervous system, the theory treats synchronicity, telepathy, and psychokinesis as ordinary — if statistically subtle — consequences of a shared, nonlocal "world of experience."

This is the part of the framework furthest from mainstream science on its own terms: the book leans on parapsychology research (particularly Dean Radin's) that most physicists, and many parapsychologists, consider unresolved or contested.

2017
Chromogravity & Electrogravity

Gravity depends on what you're weighing

If gravity is a side effect of charge, then it should scale with quark count (chromogravity) rather than mass, plus a much shorter-range contribution from electric charge (electrogravity). That means Newton's "constant" isn't constant — it should read slightly differently for iron, water, and lead. The book tests this directly against a century of Cavendish, pendulum, and free-fall experiments.

Read the core idea

Nuclear binding energy means a kilogram of iron and a kilogram of water contain slightly different numbers of protons and neutrons. If gravity tracks quark count rather than mass directly, every element should have its own gravitational constant, differing by roughly 0.1% among common metals and about 1% between iron and hydrogen.

That's a testable, falsifiable claim, and the book's later chapters are essentially a data project: reanalyzing historical measurements of big-G, the Eötvös experiment, pendulum trials, and free-fall drops, arguing that the scatter researchers usually treat as measurement error actually tracks chemical composition.

Slow down for these five

Ideas worth a plain-language pass

A handful of concepts do most of the work across all six theories. Open any of these for the short version, without the equations.

What is a superposition, here?

Not "everywhere at once" — a cloud of near-identical worlds.

Read the explanation

In most descriptions of quantum mechanics, a superposition is treated as a single particle somehow occupying multiple states at once, which is famously hard to picture. This framework reframes it: think of every possible state of the entire universe as a point in an enormous space of possibilities. A superposition is a small cloud of nearby points — worlds so similar that nothing yet distinguishes them.

Consciousness sits inside every world in that cloud simultaneously. A measurement doesn't pick a hidden value out of thin air; it shrinks the cloud down until only the worlds consistent with what you just observed remain.

What is granular space?

Space built from overlapping spherical surfaces, not a smooth backdrop.

Read the explanation

Every particle — mostly the virtual particles constantly appearing and vanishing in the vacuum — carries its own small, two-dimensional spherical surface, sized to its energy. Pack enough of these "elementary spaces" together and their overlaps weave into the smooth, three-dimensional space we move through.

Near a massive object, more of these surfaces pile up, which means more possible paths lead toward it. That surplus of paths is what shows up, at everyday scales, as gravitational pull — geometry and matter turn out to be the same stuff.

How does black hole entropy drive expansion?

Entropy grows as mass², so the universe has to keep pace.

Read the explanation

A black hole's entropy is proportional to the square of its mass, so as black holes across the universe slowly grow, total entropy grows even faster. The proposal is that the average entropy density of the universe never changes — it's held constant by the expansion of space itself.

Because entropy grows with the square of mass, the expansion needed to keep density constant also has to accelerate — matching the observed acceleration without needing to invoke a separate dark energy.

Consciousness as a relation, not a substance

A feeling is the set of every experience of it, not a signal in a brain.

Read the explanation

Borrowing Gottlob Frege's trick for defining numbers — "three" is the set of every group of three things — this theory defines a feeling the same way. The warmth of the color red isn't stored anywhere; it's the set of every experience of red that has ever happened, which is why it can feel the same to two different people.

That "world of experience" isn't confined to one skull, which is the theoretical basis the book uses for treating telepathy and psychokinesis as measurable, if statistically weak, phenomena rather than impossibilities.

Composition-dependent gravity

A kilogram of lead and a kilogram of water shouldn't fall quite alike.

Read the explanation

If gravity tracks the number of quarks in an object rather than its mass, then two objects of identical mass but different chemical makeup — say, iron versus water — should experience very slightly different gravitational pull, because nuclear binding energy means they don't contain quite the same number of protons and neutrons per kilogram.

This directly contradicts Einstein's equivalence principle, one of general relativity's most tested foundations. The book's later chapters reanalyze historical big-G measurements, pendulum trials, and free-fall drops looking for exactly this signature.

About the author

Sky Darmos

Born on an olive plantation in Greece to a Greek father and a German mother, Darmos traces the framework's first ideas to a solar-system drawing in the sand on a New Year's night, and to two specific journeys: a train platform in Pforzheim, Germany, in 2004, and a car ride to a beach near Skala, Greece, in 2005.

Between 2007 and 2012 the work paused entirely for an unrelated six-year detour into etymology — a comparative study of German and Chinese cognates — before returning to physics in Macao in 2012. The book has been revised continuously since a first short draft in 2014, moving through what the author calls six phases: conceptual, computational, and then four rounds of gathering evidence, from cosmology through gravity, particle physics, and astrophysics.

The manuscript is dedicated to Sir Roger Penrose, cited throughout as the primary influence, alongside John Archibald Wheeler, Paul Davies, and several independent gravity researchers whose experimental data the book reanalyzes directly.
