Thirty problems, and where each is argued

The book's title page lists thirty long-standing problems it says it resolves. This index gives each one its own row: the book's one-line answer, the chapter it belongs to, and — kept separate — how much of that answer this site has unpacked so far. Where the site hasn't unpacked a problem in a section of its own, the row says so.

A note on what's below. Every resolution here is the book's own claim, not an independent verification, and many contradict mainstream physics — the six theory pages carry the specific caveats. The coverage line under each row describes this site, not the argument: it says how much a page here unpacks the claim, not how well the claim holds up.

Cosmology The accelerated expansion problem Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion Black hole entropy grows with the square of mass, and expansion exists to keep entropy density constant — acceleration falls out for free. See Entropic Expansion. ● Covered in a section of this site
Cosmology The Hubble tension Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion There's no single expansion rate to disagree about — the rate itself depends on local entropy, so different methods were never measuring the same thing. The book states this in its summary table with little worked argument: the supporting machinery is the point that a region's local Hubble value depends on local black-hole growth, and it doesn't work through the early-versus-late discrepancy itself. Cosmology Non-Copernican structures Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion Hartnett's concentric shells in galaxy distributions were first read as an effect of observation centred on Earth, linked to the Sun's galactic orbit. The book then raised its own objections, and its current summary answer is baryon acoustic oscillations plus an emergent universe, with observation-driven retro-causality kept for the north–south asymmetry seen in galaxies and the microwave background. The BAO fit uses an initial temperature chosen to match the observed scale. See Entropic Expansion and the entanglement section. ● Covered in a section of this site — under the site's strongest cosmology caveat
Cosmology Fluctuations in the CMB Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion The book attributes them to primordial black holes formed when an exotic-matter Wigner crystal collapsed, each lattice point encapsulated by ordinary matter that flips its net sign. Without primordial holes, it argues, there is no expansion to stretch and amplify small random fluctuations, and without state reduction they would stay in superposition. It expects no strong primordial gravitational-wave signature, and says its future-observation principle explains the cold spots and hemispheric asymmetry but not these small density fluctuations. It also tests whether the acoustic-oscillation structure can be sound waves from density differences left by exotic-matter black holes (December 2025). The answer is assembled from several passages, though the book's summary table states it plainly. See Entropic Expansion and the full-age section. ◐ Touched on — the collapse mechanism is on the Entropic Expansion page, though not framed as an answer to this problem
Cosmology The M–sigma relation Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion Every supermassive black hole grew from a primordial black hole on the same clock as its host galaxy, so the two were never going to be independent. The book adds that galaxy-formation models need primordial holes to explain both very early galaxy formation and the correlation between hole size and host size, but it has no dedicated derivation of the relation. See Entropic Expansion. ◐ Touched on — no section of its own; the book itself argues it only briefly
Cosmology The missing local expansion Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion Expansion is entropic and non-homogeneous, so it's far too small to detect near a dense, low-entropy region like our own galaxy. See Entropic Expansion. ● Covered in a section of this site
Cosmology The quasar redshift problem Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion The same larger-distance relationship is applied to quasars: the most distant one charted on the Entropic Expansion page (z = 7.54) comes out near 299 billion light-years instead of 13. The book's first attempt at quasar redshifts, in 2014, was later folded into this derivation. See Entropic Expansion. ◐ Touched on — no section of its own
Cosmology The supernova luminosity problem Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion Intergalactic distances are around three times larger than assumed, which restores the inverse-square law and explains why remote supernovae looked dim. The book's test compares its distances with light-travel distances, and the result depends heavily on that baseline; see the caveat on the Entropic Expansion page. See Entropic Expansion. ● Covered in a section of this site

Quantum foundations The quantum spin interpretation problem Chapters 2 and 3 · Similar-Worlds, Space Particle & Charge Mass Dualism The book has no section titled for this; the answer is assembled from several passages. Massless particles have one elementary-space component, while massive fermions are modelled as two merged elementary spaces — a two-component Dirac spinor, which is why fermions need two rotations to return to themselves and bosons one (dated 9 Sep 2025). A photon's spin is its chirality, set by the sign of the imaginary part of its complex energy. Gluons show spin-1 only because length contraction near light speed removes longitudinal modes; W and Z keep them. When elementary spaces merge, the new wavefunction generates a fresh spin ½ (in the book's account two electrons net zero and three restore ½), which the muon and tauon spin-energy calculation uses. Earlier attempts — spin from orbiting elementary-space pairs (2006) and one- versus two-dimensional spaces (2015) — were retired; the author's own summary dates the interpretation to 2006 and 2015, but the current version is the 2025 one. ○ Summarised here and on the homepage only — assembled, not stated in one place; the related spin-energy calculation is on the Charge Mass Dualism page
Quantum foundations Quasicrystals Chapter 2 · Similar-Worlds Quasicrystals with five-fold symmetry are regarded as unreachable by classical local growth. Following Penrose, the book says they must grow in superposition and settle into their lowest-energy state when the wavefunction collapses — easy if superposition can last until a measurement, hard under Penrose's one-graviton criterion. It offers this as support for consciousness-caused collapse over objective reduction, and as a possible test of collapse timing: irregularities in the patterns might scale with the superposition they grew in. It bears on the measurement problem and the decoherence debate. The idea returns in a later chapter as an analogy for how neural pathways are “chosen” (see Conscious Set Theory for the quantum-oracle picture). The book gives it one paragraph. Related: the Similar-Worlds page on Orch-OR. ○ Summarised here and on the homepage only — the book gives it a single paragraph

Gravity The black hole information paradox Chapter 4 · Entropic Expansion No true event horizon ever forms, and photons carry no gravity to lose — so there's nothing for Hawking radiation to carry away. See Entropic Expansion. ● Covered in a section of this site
Gravity The neutron star–black hole mass gap Chapter 6 · Chromogravity The gap shrinks to about a third of its size once masses are recalculated from quark count instead of assuming the equivalence principle. See Chromogravity. ● Covered in a section of this site
Gravity Chronology protection Chapter 6 · Chromogravity Space can't bend into a wormhole loop, because nothing ever truly bends — granular density differences stand in for curvature, and no true horizon forms. See Chromogravity. ● Covered in a section of this site
Gravity The singularity problem Chapters 4 and 6 · Entropic Expansion, Chromogravity There's no infinitely curved starting point: the "Big Bang" is a low-density border, and a perfectly uniform early universe would curve nothing however dense it was. For black holes, the book's summary answer is different: infinite time dilation freezes a collapsing star before a singularity or true event horizon forms, and it gives the reason as the preferred (absolute) frame it says quantum mechanics needs. See Entropic Expansion, and Chromogravity for the tension with the light-cone picture. ● Covered in a section of this site
Gravity The trouble with big-G Chapter 6 · Chromogravity Newton's constant was never universal — measured gravity depends on the chemical composition of whatever you're weighing. See Chromogravity. ● Covered in a section of this site
Particle physics The generations of matter Chapter 3 · Space Particle & Charge Mass Dualism Muons and tauons are merged elementary spaces of electrons — their masses fall out of how many electron-spaces combine. See Charge Mass Dualism. ● Covered in a section of this site
Particle physics The Koide formula Chapter 3 · Space Particle & Charge Mass Dualism The merged-electron model reproduces the Koide ratio to 99.85% — after a sequence of correction terms added one at a time, which makes the fit weaker evidence than a blind prediction would be. See Charge Mass Dualism. ● Covered in a section of this site
Particle physics The origin of mass Chapter 3 · Space Particle & Charge Mass Dualism Mass is a particle's own field energy, not an added property: an electron's self-energy matches its measured mass to seven figures, and heavier particles are built by merging elementary spaces — masses the Higgs mechanism leaves as free parameters. See Charge Mass Dualism. ● Covered in a section of this site
Particle physics The hierarchy problem Chapter 3 · Space Particle & Charge Mass Dualism Gravity looks vanishingly weak because it's diluted by the sheer density of the quantum vacuum's virtual particles — not because it's a separate, mismatched force. See Space Particle Dualism. ● Covered in a section of this site
