Sky Darmos, and how this guide reads him

One author, one manuscript revised in the open for more than a decade, and six theories that grew out of it. This page covers where the ideas came from, who Darmos credits, what readers and researchers have written to him — and, just as important, what this companion site is and is not claiming.

About the author

Sky Darmos

Born on an olive plantation in Greece to a Greek father and a German mother, Darmos traces the framework's first ideas to a solar-system drawing in the sand on a New Year's night, and to two specific journeys: a train platform in Pforzheim, Germany, in 2003, and a car ride to a beach near Skala, Greece, in 2005.

Between 2007 and 2012 the work paused entirely for an unrelated six-year detour into etymology — a comparative study of German and Chinese cognates — before returning to physics in Macao in 2012. He has revised the manuscript continuously since a first short draft in 2014.

Six phases of revision

How the manuscript was built

Darmos describes the book's development as six phases: a conceptual one, a computational one, and then four rounds of gathering evidence.

That habit of dating and revising his own work in the open is one of its most distinctive features, and it is the reason this site has a chronology at all: every theory's development notes carry dated entries, and the timeline merges them into one sequence.

Influences and dedication

Whom the book answers to

The manuscript is dedicated to Sir Roger Penrose, cited throughout as Darmos's primary influence.

Alongside Penrose he credits John Archibald Wheeler, Paul Davies, and several independent gravity researchers whose experimental data he reanalyzes directly. The book also opens with five epigraphs, quoted here, that between them frame the question the theories try to answer: what role observers, consciousness and information play in physics.

"The atoms or elementary particles themselves are not real; they form a world of potentialities rather than one of things or facts."

Werner Heisenberg

"It was not possible to formulate the laws of quantum mechanics in a fully consistent way without reference to consciousness."

Eugene Wigner

"We are not only participators in creating the here and near, but also the far away and long ago."

John Archibald Wheeler

"A scientific world-view which does not profoundly come to terms with the problem of conscious minds can have no serious pretensions of completeness."

Roger Penrose

"I regard consciousness as fundamental. I regard matter as derivative from consciousness."

Max Planck

About this guide

What this site is, and isn't

Elementary Spaces is an independent companion guide to The Complete Guide to Quantum Gravity by Sky Darmos. It is not peer-reviewed, not affiliated with any research institution, and not an assessment of the theory's validity.

It is a map for navigating what Darmos himself proposes: it says what he claims, where he argues it, and how the pieces connect. Where this site has simplified or regrouped his material, it tries to say so. Whether the claims hold up is a question for the evidence, and for readers.

Where to go next. The six theories are the core of the site. The problems index lists the thirty questions on Darmos's title page. The timeline merges every dated development note. Concepts introduces the shared vocabulary, and the formulae reference collects the equations.
